Country’s external debt nearly doubles in a decade

Published on November 06, 2026

What happened

Pakistan’s total external debt almost doubled over a ten-year period, reaching $135.1 billion in 2025 compared to $68.7 billion in 2015, according to reporting by Dawn citing the World Bank’s International Debt Report 2026. On a broader scale, external debt across low- and middle-income economies rose by 5.5 percent during the same year to reach a record total of $9.4 trillion.

Key facts

According to Dawn, the World Bank data reveals key details regarding Pakistan’s obligations and wider global debt developments:

  • Pakistan’s total external obligations comprise $107.076 billion in public and publicly guaranteed debt, $17.58 billion in private non-guaranteed debt, and $10.45 billion in short-term borrowing.
  • China is Pakistan's largest individual creditor with $22.4 billion outstanding, followed by the World Bank with $19.2 billion and the Asian Development Bank with $16.83 billion.
  • During 2025, China extended repayment deadlines on $2 billion in loans to Pakistan, while the International Monetary Fund provided $2.3 billion in disbursements under its Extended Fund Facility and Resilience and Sustainability Facility arrangements.
  • Pakistan's borrowing terms showed that official creditor loans averaged a 3.6 percent interest rate with a 16-year maturity, whereas private credit carried an average interest rate of 8.6 percent with a three-year repayment period.
  • Globally, debt owed by developing economies to private creditors reached $5 trillion in 2025, while total debt service payments across these nations grew to $1.4 trillion.

Why it matters

The statistics highlight ongoing financial pressures facing developing nations. The external debt-to-gross national income ratio for the Middle East, North Africa, Afghanistan, and Pakistan region expanded from 26.7 percent to 29.4 percent in 2025, marking its largest single-year climb. While borrowing costs escalated for the poorest nations—with International Development Association-eligible countries facing average interest rates of 8.2 percent from private creditors—stronger gross national income growth of 5.1 percent across developing economies helped long-term debt flows turn into a net positive inflow of $45.4 billion in 2025.
