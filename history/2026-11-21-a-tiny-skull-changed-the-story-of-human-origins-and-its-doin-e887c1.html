A tiny skull changed the story of human origins, and it’s doing it again

Published on November 21, 2026

In 1924, a skull the size of an orange provided the first fossil proof that early humans evolved in Africa. Known as the Taung Child, this specimen serves as the original reference specimen, or holotype, for Australopithecus africanus. Recent research has pinned the age of the fossil to between 2.96 and 3.03 million years old. For decades, researchers categorized dozens of southern African fossils under the same species label using vague qualitative descriptors.

What Scientists Found

An international research team led by Gabriele Macho from Vrije Universiteit Brussel used advanced analytical techniques to re-evaluate the fossil evidence. Their study combined uranium-lead dating, 3D dental morphometrics of the boundary between enamel and dentine in first molars, braincase growth modeling, and fossilized cave pollen.

  • Taung's molar shape sat at the outer edge of its assigned species cluster, positioned between specimens Sts 18 and SK 61.
  • Fossils long grouped with Taung, such as MLD 2 from Makapansgat and Sts 24 from Sterkfontein, looked nothing like the original reference fossil.
  • Braincase modeling showed Taung could have grown into adult shapes like Sts 5 and Sts 60, but three other assigned fossils could not be explained by the same growth patterns.
  • Pollen samples revealed ancient Podocarpus trees, sedges, and grasses, showing the location was once a wetter mixture of woodland and savanna compared to the modern dry environment.

Why It Matters

The study, published in the journal npj Palaeoecosystems, indicates that early human evolution was driven by climate dynamics rather than a simple lineage. Between 4 and 2.5 million years ago, climate oscillations driven by orbital cycles caused local habitats to expand and contract. These shifting environments repeatedly split early human populations apart and brought them back together. As a result, many fossils previously labeled as Australopithecus africanus may belong to different species, proving that the human family tree was a complex, tangled bush.
