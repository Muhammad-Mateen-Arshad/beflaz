Fossilized Jaw And Teeth Of Giant Elephant, Believed To Be Of Palaeoloxodon Species Found In MP's Raisen

Published on November 21, 2026

The Discovery

A remarkable prehistoric find began when local children digging soil at Patai Ghat along the Narmada River in Madhya Pradesh's Raisen district stumbled upon an unusual stone-like object. Initially venerated by villagers who viewed it as a divine occurrence, the site was soon secured by Raisen Collector Rohit Sisonia, who alerted the Archaeological Survey of India (ASI). Following the discovery on November 10, an expert team supervised by palaeontologist Prof. Vijay Sathe from Pune and Dr. Anjali Trivedi from Lucknow excavated the fossilized remains and transported them to Bhopal for examination.

What Scientists Found

The excavated fossil consists of a large jaw and high, prominent teeth belonging to an ancient elephant, tentatively linked to the Palaeoloxodon species. The jaw spans approximately 75 centimeters in length and 25 centimeters in width, featuring a broad lower chin structure. The jaw bones are arranged in seven to nine parallel rows, exhibiting 11 tooth-like projections on one side and seven on the other.

Geological evaluation indicates the yellow soil layer holding the fossil belongs to the Baneta and Sohagpur Formation, which spans between 24,000 and 500,000 years ago. Experts place the age of the specimen at approximately 400,000 to 500,000 years old, likely originating from the Middle Pleistocene era of the Quaternary Period. Prehistoric elephants from this era stood 4 to 5 meters (13 to 16 feet) tall and weighed between 18 and 22 tons.

Why It Matters

The Narmada Valley is a major focal point for prehistoric research, having previously yielded dinosaur remains, ancient egg-like structures, human skulls in Hathnora, and elephant tusks in Surajkund. The surrounding Raisen district is also globally recognized for housing the UNESCO heritage site of Bhimbetka and its ancient rock paintings.

The fossil will undergo initial testing in Bhopal, followed by detailed joint studies by the ASI, Dr. Trivedi's team, and Prof. Sathe. Researchers may send the specimen to the Birbal Sahni Institute in Lucknow or conduct further chemical analysis in Pune. Scientists anticipate the discovery will provide crucial insight into the evolutionary environment of the Narmada Valley between 2.5 million and 12,000 years ago, with the possibility of unearthing further fossilized ribs and body parts at the excavation site.
