‘I Was Jumping Up And Down’: Scientists Believe They’ve Just Uncovered The First Dinosaur Blood Ever Found

Published on November 19, 2026

The Discovery

Researchers at Curtin University in Australia believe they have identified remnants of dinosaur blood inside a 115-million-year-old fossilized leg bone. The specimen belonged to an Aratasaurus museunacionali, a small, two-legged carnivore that roamed during the Cretaceous period. Originally excavated from a Brazilian mine in 2008, this young dinosaur was approximately four years old, 10 feet long, and weighed 75 pounds when it died. A high abundance of local microbes helped preserve the animal's carcass exceptionally well.

What Scientists Found

To examine the specimen, scientists sliced sections of the hind leg fossil and viewed them under a microscope. Inside the bone's vascular canals, they spotted microscopic, blob-like forms measuring around 23 micrometers long by 20 micrometers wide, complete with sunken centers that resembled red blood cells. The team conducted nearly 30 distinct tests, including firing lasers at ground-up bone samples. This technique uncovered degraded remnants of heme, an essential component of the oxygen-carrying protein hemoglobin. Because the heme was degraded, researchers determined it was truly ancient. They also tested surrounding sediment to rule out sample contamination, obtaining very different chemical results.

Why It Matters

Because soft tissues like blood cells break down rapidly after death, finding confirmed dinosaur blood has challenged paleontologists for decades. If verified, analyzing ancient blood cells could reveal how these creatures adapted to their environments. Published in the journal Proceedings of the National Academy of Sciences on November 2, this investigation follows at least 14 previous attempts by researchers to definitively identify red blood cells in dinosaur fossils.

Scientific Debates

Despite the comprehensive study, some external experts express skepticism. Paleontologist Mary Schweitzer observed that the structures are larger than the blood cells of living avian and crocodilian relatives and lack visible cell nuclei. Paleobiologist Maria McNamara also suggested further testing to eliminate other potential heme sources. However, lead author Kliti Grice stands by the conclusions, arguing that cell shapes and chemistry likely altered over millions of years as the bone mineralized.
