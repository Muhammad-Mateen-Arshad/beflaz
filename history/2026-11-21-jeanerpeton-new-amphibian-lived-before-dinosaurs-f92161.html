Jeanerpeton: “New” Amphibian Lived Before Dinosaurs

Published on November 21, 2026

A fossil that remained hidden in a Chicago museum drawer for years has finally revealed its true identity. Originally collected by an amateur fossil hunter and donated to the Field Museum of Natural History, the specimen was long assumed to belong to a previously known group of ancient creatures. However, a detailed re-examination has proved that the fossil represents a completely distinct species that inhabited the Earth around 309 million years ago, long before dinosaurs ever existed.

The Discovery

When scientists first examined the fossil, they categorized it as Amphibamus grandiceps. Historically, researchers placed similar specimens into this category without fully distinguishing their unique anatomical differences. A new study led by student researchers Peyton Kolbarg and Allison Sefkowicz, along with co-author Kam So, conducted a thorough reanalysis of the creature's skull and skeleton. Their findings, published in the Journal of Vertebrate Paleontology, provided enough evidence to officially remove the fossil from its previous classification and designate it as a brand-new genus and species.

What Scientists Found

The newly identified creature has been named Jeanerpeton mazonensis, which means "Jean’s creeping creature from Mazon Creek." The name honors the mothers of both lead student authors, both of whom are named Jean. As an early relative of modern amphibians, the animal possessed a short, stocky body with an elongated overall shape, blending traits reminiscent of both frogs and salamanders.

During the Carboniferous period, the region surrounding modern-day Chicago was a warm, swampy river delta. Rapid sediment accumulation at the Mazon Creek site allowed delicate anatomical structures to be preserved rather than lost during fossilization. As a result, the fossil retains extraordinary fine details, including:

  • Scales along the back
  • Miniature belly ribs known as gastralia
  • Clear imprints of the eyes
  • Impressions of body contours and toe pads

Why It Matters

The reclassification of Jeanerpeton mazonensis highlights how much remains to be learned about early amphibian evolution. Beyond resolving a historical misidentification, the discovery demonstrates that scientific breakthroughs do not always require fresh field excavations. Existing museum collections can still hold entirely unknown prehistoric species waiting to be uncovered through modern research.
