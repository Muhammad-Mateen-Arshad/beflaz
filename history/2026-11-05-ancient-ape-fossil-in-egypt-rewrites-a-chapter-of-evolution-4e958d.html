Ancient ape fossil in Egypt rewrites a chapter of evolution

Published on November 05, 2026

The Discovery

Researchers from the Mansoura University Vertebrate Paleontology Center and the University of Southern California have identified the first definitive fossil ape ever recorded in North Africa. The remains were uncovered at Wadi Moghra in northern Egypt, a location known for preserving animal life from the Early Miocene. Named Masripithecus moghraensis, this ancient primate lived between 17 and 18 million years ago.

What Scientists Found

As detailed in the journal Science, the research team examined the shape of the animal's preserved jaw and teeth to evaluate its position within the primate family tree. Dental and jaw features can retain vital information about evolutionary relationships even when most of the skeleton is missing. Their analysis places the species near a lineage that includes the last common ancestor of all living apes, a group that encompasses gibbons, great apes, and humans.

Why It Matters

Prior to this finding, North African locations had yielded early monkey remains, but a lack of clear ape fossils created a geographic gap between older discoveries in southern Africa and younger finds in Eurasia. Because northern Africa sits near routes connecting Africa and Eurasia, the presence of an ape there makes it more plausible that the region played a role in early movements of apes between continents.

While one specimen cannot reveal the exact origin of the common ancestor of modern apes or prove whether the Egyptian creature was a direct ancestor or part of an extinct branch, it fills a missing piece in the fossil record. The discovery gives paleontologists a concrete reason to investigate less-studied sites across northern Africa and the Middle East to determine if these animals were part of a more widespread group.
