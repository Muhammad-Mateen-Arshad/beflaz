A New Dinosaur Sat in a London Museum for 25 Years, Mislabeled as Iguanodon

Published on November 04, 2026

The Discovery

Fossils unearthed in 2001 at Ewhurst Works in Surrey, England, were originally misidentified as Iguanodon and donated to the Natural History Museum. The specimens remained preserved in excellent condition in museum storage for 25 years. Researchers re-evaluated the remains in 2026 using updated scientific perspectives, realizing the fossils actually represented a completely distinct species named Paludohadros proudlovii. This represents only the fifth Iguanodontian species identified. The name Paludohadros translates to "swamp" in Latin, reflecting its ancient habitat, while proudlovii honors Will Proudlove, a passionate dinosaur enthusiast who worked at the fossil site prior to his death.

What Scientists Found

The individual preserved in the clay pit was a juvenile that inhabited the swampy Weald Clay Formation roughly 125 million years ago during the Early Cretaceous period. At the time of its death, the young creature was around 16 feet long and weighed up to 2.2 tons, comparable in size to a modern rhinoceros. Paleontologists estimate that adult members of the species could have reached double that size. Its low stance allowed it to easily feed on diverse wetland plants that thrived in the seasonally flooded ecosystem.

Why It Matters

For more than a century, researchers frequently categorized various newly found Iguanodontian fossils under the single name Iguanodon. Reanalyzing museum collections has allowed scientists to correct these historical misclassifications and better understand ancient biodiversity. This particular discovery also sheds light on prehistoric interactions, as the fossils were located just 328 feet from a Baryonyx, a large predatory dinosaur found in 1983. Because the Baryonyx specimen contained a young iguanodontian in its stomach contents, experts believe the juvenile Paludohadros proudlovii did not die of natural causes, having been either hunted or scavenged by the predator.
