At Least Seven Hunts Over 3,000 Years Reveal Rare Texas Bison Jump

Published on November 22, 2026

The Discovery

In November 2021, Texas Historical Commission steward Rick Day and his partner Susan Day were scouting Caprock Canyons State Park near Quitaque for a school science trip when they spotted bison bones eroding from a dirt cliff slope. The location was named the Nighthawk site after the nesting nightjar birds observed by the team. Because rapid erosion threatened the area—destroying an estimated 70 percent of the site—archaeologists conducted test excavations in 2023 and completed a larger salvage dig in the spring of 2024.

What Scientists Found

A study led by geoarchaeologist August "Gus" Costa of Rice University published the findings in the journal American Antiquity. By analyzing 27 radiocarbon dates from bison bone collagen and charcoal, researchers determined that hunters used the same cliff location at least seven times over roughly 3,000 years, spanning from about 3,500 to 400 years ago.

The excavations revealed several details about the hunting practices and tools used at the site:

  • Drive Tactics: Hunters on foot stampeded bison off a cliff about 20 meters high, requiring large-scale human cooperation to execute the jump and process the carcasses before the meat spoiled.
  • Weaponry Evolution: Finds ranged from older Late Archaic dart points to later Scallorn and Washita arrow points used after the arrival of the bow.
  • Material Connections: Stone tools included local Tecovas chert and Ogallala quartzite, alongside Alibates flint from roughly 130 kilometers away and Edwards chert from Central Texas, about 500 to 600 kilometers to the southeast.

Why It Matters

Communal bison jumps were previously best known from the northern Plains, leading some researchers to hypothesize that the Southern Plains lacked appropriate cliffs for such techniques. The discovery at Nighthawk refutes that idea by providing rare, precisely dated physical evidence of repeated jump hunting in the Texas Panhandle. Additionally, the site offers the first confirmed instance of Late Prehistoric bison jumping in the southern Plains, challenging long-standing assumptions about regional hunting traditions across time.
