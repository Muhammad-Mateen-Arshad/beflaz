The ‘hell chicken’ dinosaur was a real terror — to fruit

Published on November 13, 2026

The Discovery

In 2011, a fossil collector working in eastern Montana found a significant portion of a fossilized dinosaur skeleton and alerted David Evans, a paleontologist at Canada's Royal Ontario Museum. Over the following years, collectors and researchers uncovered four additional partial skeletons across Montana, North Dakota, and South Dakota.

The creature was identified as a member of the oviraptorosaurs, a birdlike subgroup of beaked theropod dinosaurs that lived during the Cretaceous Period, more than 65 million years ago. Weighing roughly 12 kilograms—about the size of a modern turkey—the species was named Infernogallus jamesi, or the "hell chicken." Its name reflects its poultry-like features and its discovery in the Hell Creek geological formation.

What Scientists Found

Research on the skull, jaw joints, palate, and muscle layout of Infernogallus showed that it chewed differently than its relatives. Rather than moving its jaws up and down, it moved its jaw forward and backward. Findings reported on October 10 in Nature Communications by Evans, Greg Funston of Stony Brook University, and their colleagues highlighted several key insights:

  • Parrot-like mechanics: The dinosaur's sliding jaw motion closely mirrors the feeding techniques used by present-day fruit-eating parrots.
  • Specialized adaptation: This specimen provides the first evidence of a non-avian dinosaur evolving specific physical adaptations designed for eating fruit.
  • Seed processing: The animal used its tongue and shifting jaw motions to strip flesh from large seeds.

Why It Matters

The discovery provides physical evidence regarding angiosperms—plants that produce flowers and fruit—which were rapidly diversifying throughout the Cretaceous Period. The specialized traits of Infernogallus support the idea that Cretaceous animals were coevolving alongside these expanding plant species.

Ultimately, the finding demonstrates that researchers have been missing major pieces of the puzzle regarding dinosaur ecology right before the Cretaceous extinction. It shows that dinosaurs were far more diverse than previously thought, suggesting that other hell chicken relatives or dinosaur groups may have regularly eaten fruit as well.
