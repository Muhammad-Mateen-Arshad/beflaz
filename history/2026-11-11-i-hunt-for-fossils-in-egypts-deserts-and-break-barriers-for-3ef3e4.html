I hunt for fossils in Egypt’s deserts and break barriers for other women

Published on November 11, 2026

In 2010, Saana El Sayed was studying geology at Mansoura University in Egypt when a lecture by paleontologist Hesham Sallam changed her career path. Sallam, founder of the Mansoura University Vertebrate Paleontology Center (MUVP), highlighted Egypt's rich fossil history and the need to train local scientists. Despite initial resistance from her conservative family and a lack of female role models in desert field research, El Sayed joined the team, eventually completing her PhD and taking on a leadership role at the center.

The Discovery

Over more than 40 field expeditions across Egypt's deserts, El Sayed and her colleagues have unearthed key prehistoric remains. Significant discoveries include:

  • The remains of a giant two-metre marine catfish in Wadi El-Hitan, dating back about 37 million years.
  • The bones of Mansourasaurus in the Dakhla Oasis, a long-necked, plant-eating dinosaur from roughly 80 million years ago.
  • A rich collection of 62.2-million-year-old fish fossils at the Qreiya 3 site in the Eastern Desert.

What Scientists Found

The fish fossils at Qreiya 3 offered a clear window into marine life roughly four million years after the asteroid impact associated with the extinction of non-avian dinosaurs. The team identified ancient relatives of modern ocean species, including tuna and jacks. Finding these species in rocks of that age proves that modern marine communities began forming remarkably quickly following the mass extinction.

Why It Matters

The discovery of Mansourasaurus provided crucial evidence for evolutionary geography, as land vertebrate fossils from the final 30 million years of the Cretaceous are rare in mainland Africa. Its links to titanosaurs in southern Europe and eastern Asia showed that land animals were still traveling between northern Africa and Eurasia late in the Cretaceous period. Beyond paleontology, El Sayed's work helped break cultural barriers, establishing a clear path for future generations of Egyptian women in scientific field work.
