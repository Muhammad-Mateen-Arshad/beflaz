Scientists Find Traces of Dinosaur Blood in 115-Million-Year-Old Fossil

Published on November 05, 2026

The Discovery

In 2008, fossilized leg bones from a meat-eating dinosaur known as Aratasaurus museunacionali were excavated from a mine in northeastern Brazil. The animal was approximately ten feet long and around four years old when it died. Nearly two decades after the excavation, an international research team led by scientists at Curtin University in Australia published findings on November 2 in the journal Proceedings of the National Academy of Sciences, revealing the preservation of microscopic traces linked to dinosaur blood.

What Scientists Found

Using powerful microscopes to inspect tiny internal channels where blood vessels once ran, researchers discovered numerous structures resembling red blood cells. Chemical testing further identified signatures of heme, an iron-containing molecule that forms a vital part of oxygen-transporting hemoglobin. While past research has occasionally suggested the presence of blood cells in fossils, this study delivers direct chemical evidence connecting the microscopic shapes to blood-related compounds.

Scientists believe a rare set of circumstances made this preservation possible: the dinosaur was likely buried quickly in a low-oxygen environment with high microbial activity. Later, a mineral called hausmannite formed around the microscopic features, helping retain their shape and chemical signatures. However, outside researchers have voiced caution. Paleontologist Mary Schweitzer of North Carolina State University noted that the structures differ in shape from those expected in reptiles and birds, though she praised the study's chemical analysis.

Why It Matters

Although no intact blood or dinosaur DNA was recovered, finding preserved biological traces opens new possibilities for understanding ancient creatures. Study leader Kliti Grice, a professor at Curtin University, explained that these findings could help scientists investigate how dinosaur blood evolved, how their bodies processed oxygen, and how their metabolic and activity levels operated. Furthermore, Martin Van Kranendonk, head of the School of Earth and Planetary Sciences at Curtin University, noted that learning how organic molecules survive across millions of years could aid researchers searching for evidence of ancient life elsewhere in the universe.
