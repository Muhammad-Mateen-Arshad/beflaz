Zimbabwe’s ancient riverbanks were hiding a 210-million-year-old dinosaur that doesn’t belong anywhere else on Earth

Published on October 21, 2026

The Discovery

Working from a houseboat floating along the shorelines of Lake Kariba in Matusadona National Park, an international team of paleontologists uncovered remarkable prehistoric fossils. Led by Jonah Choiniere following initial discoveries by local safari manager Steve Edwards, researchers from Zimbabwe, South Africa, and the United Kingdom conducted expeditions in 2017 and 2018. The remote location required the team to use the vessel as a mobile laboratory while extracting bones from 210-million-year-old red mudstone layers along ancient riverbeds.

What Scientists Found

The researchers identified a newly discovered sauropodomorph dinosaur named Musango matusadonaensis, which lived during the Late Triassic period. The partial skeleton belonged to a subadult that walked on two hind legs, measured about 4.5 meters long, and weighed approximately 220 kilograms. Because its skull was not preserved, its exact diet remains unknown, though its closest relatives were primarily plant-eaters.

In addition to Musango, the fossil beds yielded several other unique species, including:

  • Musankwa, a relative described from the same rock beds in 2024.
  • Fossilized lungfish and phytosaurs, which were ancient aquatic reptiles rarely or never seen in neighboring regions.

Why It Matters

Despite being geographically close to well-studied fossil sites in South Africa, every species collected at the Lake Kariba sites appears unique to Zimbabwe. During the Triassic period, Zimbabwe was situated further north and closer to the equator than South Africa, resulting in a warmer climate. Scientists suspect these distinct climatic conditions created invisible environmental barriers, fostering a separate ecosystem filled with conifer groves and green river corridors.

Dinosaur bones were first reported in Zimbabwe in 1916, followed by systematic research in the 1970s by Geoffrey Bond and Michael Raath before progress stalled. The recent finds, along with a reassessment of historical fossil collections stored at a museum in Bulawayo, reveal that Zimbabwe's ancient world was vastly different from its neighbors and played a far more significant role in early dinosaur history than previously understood.
