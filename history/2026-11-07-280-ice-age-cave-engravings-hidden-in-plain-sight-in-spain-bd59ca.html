280 Ice Age Cave Engravings Hidden in Plain Sight in Spain

Published on November 07, 2026

The Discovery

For decades, visitors exploring the underground passages of Cova de la Font Major in Catalonia, Spain, walked past priceless prehistoric engravings without realizing what they were looking at. Located beneath the town of Espluga de Francolí, the cave opened to visitors in 1957 and officially became a tourist site in 1993. Over the years, some tourists even etched modern graffiti directly across the ancient artwork.

The site's prehistoric importance came to light on October 30, 2019, when archaeologist Josep Maria Vergès Bosch noticed unusual wall markings mixed in with modern inscriptions inside a cave gallery called Gatoneres de Cal Palletes. Recognizing traits of Paleolithic art, Vergès Bosch and fellow researcher Míriam Salas-Altès conducted a detailed analysis of the cave, publishing their results in the journal Antiquity.

What Scientists Found

Researchers documented 280 graphic elements across five cave sectors, situated approximately 250 meters from the present entrance. Key details of the findings include:

  • 55 animal depictions, including horses, deer, wild cattle, and goat-like species.
  • Unusual representations, such as a bear with front-facing ears and a slightly open mouth, and the front half of an aurochs, an extinct species of wild cattle.
  • Abstract markings featuring parallel lines, geometric shapes, and hundreds of loop-like incisions.

Prehistoric artists created the figures directly on soft marl rock layers using incisions and scraping. They frequently incorporated natural rock contours into their designs, using geological cracks and boundaries to represent ground surfaces or water lines. Most animal figures range from 15 to 45 centimeters in length, with grooves three to seven millimeters wide.

Determining the precise age of the artwork is not specified in the source; please check the official notice. However, radiocarbon dates from deeper galleries indicate human activity between roughly 40,800 and 25,900 years ago, and researchers hope to perform uranium-series dating on a calcite layer covering one of the figures.

Why It Matters

Before this comprehensive study, researchers knew of only two tentative Upper Paleolithic cave art representations in Catalonia. This discovery transforms the region's historical record, establishing Cova de la Font Major as the third-largest known Paleolithic cave art assemblage along the Mediterranean side of the Iberian Peninsula.
