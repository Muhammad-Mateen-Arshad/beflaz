Fossil Footprints in Brazil Hint at a Cougar-Size Mammal That Walked Among Dinosaurs. It’s Another Sign That Some Early Beasts Grew to Impressive Sizes After All

Published on November 06, 2026

The Discovery

In 2019, quarry workers in southern Brazil uncovered a series of fossilized footprints embedded in 130-million-year-old rock deposits. The five-inch-wide imprints left behind in ancient sand dunes belonged to an unidentified prehistoric beast. The tracks stood out immediately because they were nearly double the size of any other mammal tracks previously recovered from those geological layers.

What Scientists Found

A study published in the Journal of South American Earth Sciences by Rio de Janeiro State University paleontologist Hermínio Ismael de Araújo-Júnior and colleagues examined the unusual prints. Their analysis revealed that the creature moved on four short legs with paws. Based on the footprint dimensions, researchers determined the animal was roughly the size of a modern cougar—far larger than the beasts typical of that era.

While the exact species remains unknown, paleontologist Elsa Panciroli of National Museums Scotland explained that the find provides compelling evidence of larger prehistoric mammals, joining badger-sized specimens previously uncovered elsewhere in the world.

Why It Matters

For decades, paleontologists operated under the assumption that Mesozoic mammals were exclusively tiny, cat-sized insect-eaters forced to scurry in the dark to avoid dinosaurs. However, a growing body of fossil evidence challenges this long-held belief:

  • Lisowicia: A pig-like, five-ton synapsid herbivore standing over eight feet tall at the shoulder, discovered in 205-million-year-old rock in Poland.
  • Repenomamus: A badger-like mammal from 125-million-year-old rocks in eastern China that hunted infant dinosaurs and even attacked an adult horned dinosaur three times its size.

These findings demonstrate that mammal ancestors were not strictly limited to small sizes during the age of dinosaurs. Although most ancient mammals were small species—much like modern times, where rodents, shrews, and bats make up more than half of all mammal species—larger beasts managed to thrive alongside giant reptiles.
