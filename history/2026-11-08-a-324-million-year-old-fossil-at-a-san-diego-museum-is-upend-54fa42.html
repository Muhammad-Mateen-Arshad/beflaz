A 324 million-year-old fossil at a San Diego museum is upending what we know about natural history

Published on November 08, 2026

Inside a basement vault at the San Diego Natural History Museum, a 324 million-year-old specimen cataloged as fossil No. 28852 spent decades stored under the label of an extinct crustacean known as Tesnusocaris goldichi. Originally collected four decades ago in 1985 from private property in Brewster County, Texas, by a team led by crustacean researcher Frederick Schram, the artifact was long considered an aquatic creature. However, a recent analysis by Cambridge University doctoral student Erik Thelka has transformed the scientific understanding of the artifact.

The Discovery

After seeing textbook photographs of the specimen, Thelka traveled to San Diego to inspect the fossil under specialized polarized light. His inspection uncovered distinct physical structures that prompted collection managers to issue a new label identifying it as Chosha praecursor, an ancient insect. The findings were detailed in a paper published in the journal Nature.

What Scientists Found

The examination revealed a combination of defining insect structures alongside features suited for swimming:

  • Six thoracic legs, which serve as a signature trait for insects.
  • An ovipositor, a tube-like organ used to lay eggs.
  • A terminal filament, which is a thin tail-like extension from the abdomen.
  • Multiple pairs of paddle-like abdominal legs, which likely allowed the creature—resembling a large silverfish—to travel between land and water.

Why It Matters

At 324 million years old, the specimen sits right at the younger boundary of the "hexapod gap," a 60 million-year period from 385 million to 325 million years ago—with some citing 322 million years ago—where the fossil record historically lacked insect life. Reclassifying this artifact prompted researchers to review older fossils, leading them to be identified together as the earliest uncontested insects on record. Paleontology curator Tom Deméré noted that the find provides key evidence regarding how crustaceans and insects separated in nature while highlighting the value of long-preserved museum archives.
