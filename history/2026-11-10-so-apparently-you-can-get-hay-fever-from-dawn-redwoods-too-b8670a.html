So apparently you can get hay fever from dawn redwoods too

Published on November 10, 2026

The dawn redwood, correctly spelled Metasequoia, offers a fascinating look into prehistoric plant life. Unlike most trees familiar to modern city dwellers, this ancient species was recognized from fossil remains long before living specimens were identified. Fossils dating back to the late Cretaceous period have been excavated in locations such as Japan, demonstrating that this plant thrived during the era of the dinosaurs.

The Discovery

For a long time, the plant was known only through ancient rock layers. That changed in 1945 when a living dawn redwood was found in Sichuan Province, China. This unexpected finding highlighted the species as a true "living fossil," proving that a botanical survivor from millions of years ago still exists in the modern world.

What Scientists Found

Researchers and botanists have documented several unique characteristics of the dawn redwood:

  • It is a tall tree native to central China that can reach heights of up to 40 meters.
  • Although classified as a conifer within the cypress family, it sheds its leaves along with its branches when winter arrives.
  • The foliage shifts to beautiful autumn colors during October and November.
  • It is widely cultivated today as a street tree and is frequently planted near parks and public facilities.

Why It Matters

In addition to its historic origin, the dawn redwood plays a surprising role in urban health. Its pollen shares the exact same antigenicity as cedar pollen, producing identical allergic symptoms such as a runny nose and sneezing. While cedar trees typically grow in mountainous forests, dawn redwoods are commonly located inside cities. As a result, urban allergy sufferers may actually be reacting to dawn redwood pollen, though distinguishing between the two allergy sources remains extremely difficult.
