Extinct, long-legged grasshoppers walked on water alongside dinosaurs

Published on November 25, 2026

The Discovery

While examining a collection of 99-million-year-old amber, paleontologist Chunpeng Xu from the Nanjing Institute of Geology and Paleontology in China and his colleagues uncovered remarkably unusual fossilized insects. Frozen inside three pieces of amber were wingless grasshoppers featuring short bodies and extremely long, skinny legs. Because of their unorthodox appearance, Xu initially mistook the ancient creatures for harvestmen or stick insects before realizing their true identity.

What Scientists Found

Microscopic analysis of the fossils revealed specialized adaptations that allowed these insects to walk on water nearly 100 million years ago. Key physical traits include:

  • Front legs covered with water-repellant hairs to balance on surface tension.
  • Rear lower legs featuring leaf-shaped, paddlelike structures to push the insects forward.
  • Mouthparts and a distinct lack of claws indicating the species was herbivorous, unlike modern predatory water-walking insects.

The researchers classified the insect as a newfound species named Hydrelcana zhangi, placing it in the new family Hydrelcanidae. Based on weight estimates, the insects rode on surface tension using their spread-out footprints. The researchers also found nine individuals preserved within a single piece of amber, suggesting the insects may have gathered in large groups to feed. They also moved with a different gait than modern pond skaters, which row using their middle limbs.

Why It Matters

Detailed on November 24 in Proceedings of the Royal Society B, the study reveals that moving across water evolved independently in at least three separate insect lineages. Prior to this finding, scientists only knew of two groups with this ability: water striders and their relatives, and an extinct family called Chresmodidae. Furthermore, the findings likely represent the earliest documented evidence of swarming behavior in crickets or grasshoppers.
