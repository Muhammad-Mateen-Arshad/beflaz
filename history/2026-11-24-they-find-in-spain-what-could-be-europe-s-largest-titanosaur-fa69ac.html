They find in Spain what could be Europe's largest titanosaur: it has a seven-meter neck

Published on November 24, 2026

The Discovery

Paleontologists working at the Molí del Piteu site in Isona, located in Lleida, have uncovered significant fossilized remains of an ancient giant. The heavy extraction work was finalized over roughly four hours last Wednesday using specialized machinery. A backhoe was required to move a seven-cubic-meter block of rock weighing 18 tons that contained the embedded fossils. Specialists are now carefully checking the findings to assess whether any bones suffered damage due to a partial collapse at the front section of the block.

What Scientists Found

The uncovered skeleton belonged to a massive titanosaur that inhabited the area around 70 million years ago. Estimated to weigh 15 tons, the creature possessed a neck stretching seven meters long. Paleontologists retrieved a wide array of skeletal pieces from the site, including:

  • Sections of the neck and fragments of the skull
  • Bones corresponding to the chest and front limbs
  • Elongated ribs and multiple vertebrae remaining in their original layout, indicating the carcass experienced minimal disturbance after death

Researchers also discovered numerous teeth from meat-eating dinosaurs scattered around the remains, suggesting carnivores scavenged the body. These plant-eating dinosaurs were characterized by sturdy legs and extended tails, typically moving in herds throughout coastal and wet environments.

Why It Matters

Scientists believe this individual may become the largest titanosaur specimen ever identified in Europe, potentially broadening the region's known fossil record of 12 to 18 meters. It could even prove larger than Abditosaurus, a 15- to 18-meter-long dinosaur unearthed in the same district in 2014. The research team is currently analyzing the bones to determine if they represent an entirely unclassified species.

Following a painstaking cleaning and examination process in Isona, the fossils will be exhibited at the Museu de la Conca Dellà. The team hopes to work faster than they did with Abditosaurus, which took six years to clean and ten years to formally identify. Àngel Galobart, the museum director, explained that the true importance of the find goes beyond mere dimensions, stating that the objective is to protect and raise awareness of local paleontological heritage rather than compete for size records.
