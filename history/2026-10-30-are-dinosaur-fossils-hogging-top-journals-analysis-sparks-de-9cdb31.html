Are dinosaur fossils hogging top journals? Analysis sparks debate over ‘charismatic’ finds

Published on October 30, 2026

The Discovery

When a research paper described a new species of Spinosaurus featuring a scimitar-shaped crest, it triggered widespread public attention alongside frustration from some palaeontologists. The reaction highlighted a long-standing complaint in the scientific community: high-profile scientific journals frequently favor popular, "charismatic" dinosaur fossils over less glamorous ancient organisms. To test this idea with hard data, researcher Bryan Gee from the Burke Museum of Natural History and Culture conducted an extensive study analyzing publication trends across palaeontology literature.

What Scientists Found

Gee examined more than 4,200 research papers published since 2000 across 13 journals, covering descriptions of 8,331 newly identified extinct species. His study compared top-tier publications like Science and Nature against dedicated specialist journals.

The analysis revealed a strong preference toward specific animal groups in major journals:

  • More than 50% of the fossil species published in Science and Nature were Avemetatarsalians (dinosaurs and birds) or mammaliamorphs.
  • In specialist journals, those same groups represented only 15% of described species.
  • Invertebrate species, including molluscs and arthropods, accounted for more than half of the descriptions in specialist journals.

In response to the study, an editor for Nature emphasized that editorial decisions are based independently on research quality, significance, and peer review. Representatives for Science declined to comment on the preprint, which has not undergone peer review.

Why It Matters

Researchers argue that an editorial bias toward high-profile fossils distorts research incentives across the field. Securing a paper in a prestigious journal can be critical for early-career scientists seeking tenure-track positions, which may discourage researchers from studying less famous organisms. Furthermore, past analysis by palaeobiologists Nussaïbah Raja-Schoob and Emma Dunne warned that intense pressure to publish spectacular finds can lead scientists to overlook important issues regarding a fossil's legal standing, provenance, and accessibility to other researchers.
