Scientists Say They’ve Found Preserved Dinosaur Blood in a 115-Million-Year-Old Fossil, but Not Everyone Is Convinced

Published on November 04, 2026

The Discovery

Researchers led by geochemist Kliti Grice from Curtin University in Australia analyzed a hind leg bone from Aratasaurus museunacionali, a small, two-legged, meat-eating dinosaur that lived during the Cretaceous period. The individual was estimated to be four years old at death, weighing around 75 pounds and measuring roughly 10 feet long. Originally extracted from a mine in northeastern Brazil in 2008, the fossil survived a destructive 2018 fire at Brazil's National Museum before being sent to scientists for analysis in 2024. To examine the specimen, the team prepared thin slices of the bone and used hydrochloric acid to clear away surrounding rock.

What Scientists Found

Under a high-resolution microscope, researchers spotted numerous structures in the bone's vascular canals that closely resemble red blood cells. Key findings from their analysis include:

  • Round, microscopic structures with sunken centers measuring approximately 23 micrometers long by 20 micrometers wide.
  • Two distinct chemical signatures of heme, an iron-containing component of oxygen-carrying hemoglobin.
  • Traces of an amino acid found in collagen, a structural protein in biological tissue.
  • Sediment testing around the bone that showed minimal heme presence, indicating the cellular material was not an environmental contaminant.

While these structures were longer and wider than the blood cells of birds and crocodiles, researchers suggest they may have become distorted during fossilization or were naturally shaped differently in dinosaurs.

Why It Matters

If fully confirmed, these signatures would represent the oldest preserved red blood cells containing heme ever identified in a dinosaur fossil. The scientists propose that rapid burial under water and microorganism-rich sediment slowed decay, while a manganese oxide mineral called hausmannite may have specifically helped preserve the cells. The analytical methods could help uncover hidden biological material in other ancient remains, offering deeper insight into extinct animal physiology.

However, some outside paleontologists remain cautious. Because soft biological tissues typically decompose quickly and certain minerals can visually mimic blood cells under magnification, ancient soft tissue claims remain a subject of debate within the scientific community.
