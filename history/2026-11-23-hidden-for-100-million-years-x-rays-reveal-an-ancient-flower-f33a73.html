Hidden for 100 million years: X-rays reveal an ancient flower from the age of dinosaurs

Published on November 23, 2026

The Discovery

A team of scientists from the United States and the United Kingdom has revealed a fossil flower hidden inside a rock collected over 130 years ago. Originally gathered in Kansas from the sandstone of the Dakota Formation by fossil plant pioneer Leo Lesquereux during the late nineteenth century, the sample had long been stored at the Smithsonian National Museum of Natural History in Washington, D.C. Paleobotanist Steven Manchester borrowed the specimen to examine a different flower, known as Dakotanthus, using micro-CT scanning. During the X-ray imaging process, Manchester accidentally spotted a completely entombed, miniature flower structure that had remained undetected for millions of years.

What Scientists Found

Officially named Tomlinsoniella pedicellata, the fossil measures no more than 1.5 millimeters in height and 1.2 millimeters in diameter—making it no larger than a single grain of rice. It was preserved in negative as a cavity in the rock, created when the plant fell into clay-rich sand that hardened around it before the organic material rotted away. By inverting the micro-CT scans, researchers reconstructed a three-dimensional positive view of the organism. The analysis showed a classic monocot layout made of five concentric rings with three organs each:

  • Three sepals
  • Three petals
  • Six stamens arranged in two sets of three
  • Three flask-shaped, unfused carpels

Why It Matters

Dating to the mid-Cretaceous period roughly 100 million years ago, the fossil is the earliest known direct evidence displaying the classic structural plan of a monocot flower. Comparative research indicates that the specimen most closely resembles an extinct plant within the fan palm subfamily. Monocots currently account for about 20 percent of all known flowering plants—encompassing around 70,000 living species such as orchids, bananas, yams, cereals, and lilies. While genetic evidence suggests monocots originated in the Early Cretaceous, physical specimens from the mid-Cretaceous or earlier are extremely rare, making this discovery a vital link in understanding the early evolution of modern plant life.
