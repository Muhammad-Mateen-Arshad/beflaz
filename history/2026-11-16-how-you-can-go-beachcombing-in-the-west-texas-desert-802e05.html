How you can go beachcombing in the West Texas desert

Published on November 16, 2026

While beachcombing typically brings to mind coastal shores, waves, and ocean breezes, remnants of ancient aquatic life can also be explored in the desert. Jace Tunnell, Director of Community Engagement for the Harte Research Institute at Texas A&M University-Corpus Christi, highlighted this unique perspective while trekking 60 miles across Big Bend National Park and Big Bend Ranch State Park in West Texas alongside his wife, Kathryn, during their 26th wedding anniversary.

The Discovery

The desert region of West Texas was once drastically different from its current rocky state. Heavy rainfall recently refreshed the landscape, bringing out blooming cactus flowers, thousands of butterflies, bees, and millipedes. However, the most profound discovery involves what lies preserved in the geologic record from long ago.

What Scientists Found

Millions of years ago, during the Cretaceous Period, a shallow sea covered significant portions of Texas, including the Big Bend region. Park museums display ancient marine fossils recovered from the area, giving visitors a glimpse into an aquatic past. Organisms that once inhabited these ancient desert waters include:

  • Prehistoric sharks
  • Clams
  • Ancient oysters

This ancient marine ecosystem has drawn active scientific interest; researchers from the Center for Sportfish Science and Conservation at the Harte Research Institute were recently featured on Discovery Channel's Shark Week searching for fossilized shark teeth in the Texas desert.

Why It Matters

The geologic history of Big Bend also features dinosaurs that once inhabited the region, followed by volcanic eruptions that helped sculpt its colorful rock formations, narrow canyons, and rugged mountains. The area demonstrates that beachcombing is not limited to finding items washed up on present-day shores. Instead, it can also be about discovering the traces left behind by oceans millions of years in the past.
