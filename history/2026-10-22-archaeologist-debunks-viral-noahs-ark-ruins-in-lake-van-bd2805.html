Archaeologist debunks viral Noah’s Ark ruins in Lake Van

Published on October 22, 2026

The Discovery

Recent viral online claims raised widespread speculation about submerged masonry found in Lake Van, a body of water situated 90 miles from the alleged location of Noah's Ark. Rumors suggested that an expansive 11,000-year-old urban settlement rested at depths of 75 feet beneath the surface, with internet users speculating that the site provided physical evidence of a lost prehistoric city or a biblical flood.

What Scientists Found

Archaeologist Ömer Tanyürek, who has spent over two decades researching the region in Türkiye, conducted dives in Lake Van to directly inspect the submerged ruins. His observations refute the viral claims:

  • Actual depth: The dive occurred at a depth of roughly 5 meters rather than the reported 23 meters (75 feet).
  • Structure details: The primary architectural feature examined was an oval bastion, which preliminary assessments suggest belongs to a medieval period rather than deep antiquity.
  • Fortification layers: The location appears to be a defensive fortification modified over generations. The lower foundations display Early Iron Age building styles linked to the Kingdom of Urartu, while upper sections reflect later medieval additions.
  • Geological history: Environmental data shows Lake Van experienced repeated shoreline shifts over thousands of years rather than a single catastrophic flood. There is currently no direct empirical archaeological evidence linking the site to Noah's Ark or sacred texts.

Why It Matters

Dismissing sensational timelines does not lessen the historical value of the Lake Van basin. Because the lake's water levels fluctuated over the millennia, areas that are submerged today served as dry shorelines during the Paleolithic and Neolithic eras.

Scientific research shows the basin was an important hub in ancient trade networks, with obsidian from sources like Nemrut Dağı transported across large distances during the Neolithic period. Systematic underwater surveys along these ancient shorelines may eventually reveal genuine, unstudied prehistoric human sites.
