Egypt uncovers sprawling ancient necropolis filled with coffins, gold artifacts

Published on November 22, 2026

An archaeological excavation led by the Supreme Council of Antiquities has uncovered a sprawling ancient necropolis at Kom Abu Billo, also known as Tarrana or ancient Terenouthis. Situated approximately 43 miles northwest of Cairo in the northern province of Menoufia, the site sits near the eastern entrance of Wadi el-Natrun—a region historically famous for supplying the natron salt used in Egyptian mummification. Officials described the site as unprecedented due to the unusually high concentration of sarcophagi and burials situated within an open courtyard.

The Discovery

Preliminary evaluations cited by Hisham el-Leithy, secretary-general of the Supreme Council of Antiquities, indicate that the necropolis was likely constructed for deceased members of the ancient middle class. The site's contents span several historical epochs, stretching from the end of the Egyptian Late Period (664–332 BC) through the Ptolemaic era (332–30 BCE) and into the early Roman period (30 BCE–284 CE).

What Scientists Found

Researchers unearthed a wide range of structures and grave goods, including stone and pottery sarcophagi, wooden coffin remnants, poorly preserved human remains, and traces of mummification materials. The cemetery contains vaulted mudbrick tombs along with a tomb featuring a limestone-lined interior. Key artifacts retrieved include:

  • Faience ushabti figurines intended to act as servants in the afterlife, small limestone statue heads, and terracotta figurines depicting Isis-Aphrodite.
  • Gold jewelry, gold foils, and gold tongue-shaped objects placed over or inside the mouths of the dead to allow them to speak in the afterlife.
  • Amulets, scarabs, and small figures depicting Horus and sphinxes.
  • Two offering tables and two falcon burials.

Why It Matters

The city of Terenouthis is thought to have been founded during the Old Kingdom (2700–2200 BCE) or earlier, later becoming a major exporter of natron during the Greco-Roman period. Tourism and Antiquities Minister Sherif Fathy highlighted the findings as proof of the location's historical richness. Additional fieldwork is scheduled for the site as part of Egypt's broader initiative to explore and document its ancient history.
