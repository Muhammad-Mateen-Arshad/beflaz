Evidence of red blood cells seen in dinosaur fossil from Brazil

Published on November 08, 2026

The Discovery

Researchers have uncovered what is considered the strongest evidence to date of fossilized blood cell remnants within a dinosaur bone. The fossil belongs to Aratasaurus museunacionali, a medium-sized, bipedal meat-eater that inhabited present-day Brazil approximately 115 million years ago during the Cretaceous Period. Unearthed in a gypsum mine in Ceará state in northeastern Brazil, the remains include parts of the animal's right femur, tibia, and foot. Scientists determined the dinosaur was a juvenile or young adult around 4 years old when it died, measuring about 10 feet (3.1 meters) in length.

What Scientists Found

By analyzing the vascular canals inside the leg bone, the research team identified microscopic cell-like shapes alongside related chemical signatures. Key findings detailed in the study include:

  • Numerous fossilized structures inside the bone's vascular system that closely resemble red blood cells.
  • Chemical evidence of heme, an iron-containing biomolecule linked to hemoglobin, which carries oxygen and gives blood its red color.
  • Amino acids associated with collagen, the essential structural protein that acts as scaffolding for biological tissues.

Lead author Kliti Grice, an organic and isotope geochemist at Curtin University in Australia, explained that the discovery offers a mineral and molecular echo of ancient life rather than liquid blood. The research, co-authored by paleontologist Alexander Kellner of the National Museum of Brazil, was published in the journal Proceedings of the National Academy of Sciences.

Why It Matters

Standard fossil discoveries involving hard bones and teeth reveal significant details about growth, anatomy, and evolution, but soft tissues and liquid-borne cells rarely survive. What makes this study distinct is the combined presence of physical cell-like structures, mineralization, vascular placement, and intact chemical evidence tied to heme within the same fossilized bone.

Scientists caution that the finding does not contain intact dinosaur DNA or complex genetic material. For context, the oldest confirmed DNA ever recovered dates to roughly 2 million years ago from sediment in Greenland. Even so, the discovery prompts researchers to keep exploring the boundaries of how long biomolecular traces can endure over vast geological periods.
