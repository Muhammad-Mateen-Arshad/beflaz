This Ancient ‘Hell Chicken’ Might Be the First Known Fruit-Loving Dinosaur, Revealing a Whole New Cretaceous Lifestyle

Published on November 05, 2026

When imagining the Late Cretaceous world, most people picture terrifying meat-eaters like Tyrannosaurus rex or gigantic plant-eating sauropods. However, new research shows that ancient ecosystems offered a wider menu. Scientists analyzing fossils from the Hell Creek Formation—a site spanning Montana, Wyoming, and the Dakotas—have identified a small creature that might be the first known fruit-eating dinosaur.

The Discovery

The newly described species, named Infernogallus jamesi or "hell chicken," was a turkey-sized, feathered dinosaur that weighed about 25 pounds. Belonging to a group of two-legged, beaked dinosaurs known as caenagnathids, it inhabited North America around 67 million years ago alongside giant predators. Its fossils, which include a partial skeleton and an almost complete skull, reveal a specialized lifestyle previously unsupported by direct fossil evidence.

What Scientists Found

Researchers performed CT scans on the fossils to build a digital model of the dinosaur's skull. They discovered several distinct physical traits adapted for chewing soft foods rather than meat or tough vegetation:

  • Jaw muscles capable of back-and-forth chewing, resembling structures found in modern mammals and fruit-eating birds.
  • A distinct joint that enabled the lower jaw to slide forward while feeding.
  • Internal beak ridges and grooves suited for processing soft materials such as ancient fruits.

Why It Matters

The evolution of this species coincided with a period when soft, pit-bearing fruits called drupes became larger and more plentiful. By consuming these fruits, Infernogallus jamesi may have played a key ecological role by dispersing plant seeds through its feces. This finding provides a rare link between the diversification of dinosaurs and the rise of flowering plants, demonstrating that prehistoric diets were far more varied than previously thought.
