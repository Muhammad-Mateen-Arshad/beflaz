Etruscan Tombs Reveal Rock-Carved Thrones, Painted Walls, and Decorated Pottery

Published on November 06, 2026

The Discovery

Archaeologists carrying out urgent conservation work at the Monte Abatone necropolis in Cerveteri, Italy, have revealed significant elements inside several ancient Etruscan tombs. The intervention became necessary after severe structural damage and unstable conditions put the ancient chambers at risk. Teams cleared blocked entrances and removed built-up deposits to access the burial spaces, which had previously suffered looting from illegal excavations. On November 3, 2026, Italy’s Soprintendenza Archeologia Belle Arti e Paesaggio per l’Etruria Meridionale officially announced the findings.

What Scientists Found

The investigation uncovered burial chambers showcasing complex structural features and interior decorations cut directly into the local volcanic tuff. Highlights of the archaeological discoveries include:

  • Throne-shaped seats carved directly out of the volcanic rock.
  • A chamber that preserved a large portion of its original vaulted ceiling.
  • Traces of red paint across wall surfaces, indicating decorative wall treatments. (Original appearance and chronology of the paintings: Not specified in the source; please check the official notice).
  • A nearly intact ceramic vessel known as an olla with a stamnoid shape, featuring engraved animal figures and painted red details. (The precise date of the vessel and identified animal species: Not specified in the source; please check the official notice).

Why It Matters

The Monte Abatone cemetery served ancient Caere, a major city of Etruscan civilization located northwest of Rome. According to Italy’s Ministry of Culture, this burial ground was used from the late eighth century BCE until the first century BCE. Earlier twentieth-century work mapped hundreds of tombs, while studies from 2018 to 2021 documented seventh-century BCE graves set in parallel rows. However, precise dates for the newly examined chambers remain unknown (Not specified in the source; please check the official notice).

Despite prior damage caused by tomb robbers, the surviving rock-carved furnishings, painted walls, and intact pottery offer key insights into how Etruscans organized their subterranean funerary spaces. Conservators have now stabilized the tombs, and once restoration is complete, the decorated ceramic vessel will go on public display.
