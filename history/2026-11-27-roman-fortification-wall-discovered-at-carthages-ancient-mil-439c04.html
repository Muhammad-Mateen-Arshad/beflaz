Roman Fortification Wall Discovered at Carthage’s Ancient Military Harbor

Published on November 27, 2026

The Discovery

Archaeologists working at the Punic ports in Tunisia have uncovered a Roman-era fortification wall alongside large stone blocks that may have served as a breakwater to diminish wave impact. The wall is believed to be part of the outer enclosure of the central island inside Carthage’s renowned circular military harbor. Tunisia’s National Heritage Institute (INP) announced the findings on November 26, following an excavation season that started on October 19, 2026. The research was carried out as a joint effort between the INP and China’s National Centre for Archaeology (NACA), under the supervision of the Tunisian Ministry of Cultural Affairs.

What Scientists Found

In addition to the heavy stone architectural elements, the excavation team retrieved several artifacts that highlight the long history of human occupation at the site. The findings include:

  • Bronze coins
  • Oil lamps
  • Amphorae
  • Pottery fragments

These items show continuous activity at the harbor from the Punic era through the Byzantine period. Researchers are currently documenting building foundations, historical circulation surfaces, and abandonment layers to understand the structural evolution of the maritime complex. The exact function of the stone blocks and how they tied into surrounding installations remains unconfirmed; not specified in the source; please check the official notice.

Why It Matters

Before Roman forces led by Scipio Aemilianus destroyed Carthage in 146 BCE to end the Third Punic War, the city was a dominant maritime power in the western Mediterranean. Its circular military port contained shipsheds designed for 220 vessels, with a central island that served as the commander's headquarters. Plans to rebuild Carthage were later started by Julius Caesar and finalized by Augustus in 29 BCE, transforming the location into the capital of Rome's African province.

Past excavations on the island revealed two Roman temples and a potential lighthouse. The newly discovered fortification wall helps researchers understand how Roman engineers modified the former Punic naval hub. Inscribed as a UNESCO World Heritage Site in 1979, the complex provides key evidence of how the city adapted under successive rulers, including periods under Vandal control and its eventual capture by Byzantine general Belisarius in 533 CE.
