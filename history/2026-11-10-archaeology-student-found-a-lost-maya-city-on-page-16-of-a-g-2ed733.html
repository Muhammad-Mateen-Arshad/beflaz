Archaeology student found a lost Maya city on page 16 of a Google search

Published on November 10, 2026

The Discovery

Luke Auld-Thomas, a PhD student at Tulane University, uncovered an ancient Maya city while examining online search results. Navigating to approximately page 16 of a Google search, he came across a laser scanning survey previously conducted by a Mexican environmental monitoring group. The environmental researchers were unaware that their aerial scan had recorded an ancient settlement buried under dense forest cover. Auld-Thomas and a fellow scholar subsequently named the city Valeriana, taking the name from a nearby lagoon.

What Scientists Found

The scanned dataset exposed a massive historical site in Campeche, located in southeastern Mexico. Using LiDAR—a remote sensing technique that sends laser beams from aircraft to record ground terrain—researchers bypassed the thick tree canopy to reveal hidden infrastructure. The survey identified more than 6,500 structures, including causeways, ball courts, and pyramids.

Estimates indicate that Valeriana supported a population of 30,000 to 50,000 people at its peak between roughly 750 and 850 AD. Researchers think the settlement may have served as a capital city, displaying a population density that might be second only to Calakmul among known Maya sites. Because scientists have not yet visited the location on the ground, the city currently exists only as laser mapping data, though researchers intend to conduct future field investigations.

Why It Matters

The identification of Valeriana counters long-held Western beliefs that tropical rainforests were unsuitable for large, thriving societies. Study co-author Marcello Canuto, an anthropology professor at Tulane, explained that the discovery demonstrates how tropical landscapes were intensely developed, densely settled, and filled with advanced urban hubs.

This breakthrough highlights the ongoing impact of LiDAR in modern archaeology, allowing experts to survey terrain digitally without physical deforestation. Similar applications previously unveiled major urban networks around Angkor in Cambodia in 2013 and over 60,000 features across Guatemala in 2018. Experts believe many more forgotten cities remain hidden within existing digital archives, waiting to be identified.
