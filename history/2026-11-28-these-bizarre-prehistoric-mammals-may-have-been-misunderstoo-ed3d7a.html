These bizarre prehistoric mammals may have been misunderstood for 150 years

Published on November 28, 2026

The Discovery

For generations, paleontologists viewed uintatheres—a group of giant extinct mammals with large bodies, prominent horns, and oversized canine teeth—as a primary example of sexual dimorphism. Scientists long believed that male uintatheres were much larger than females and possessed far more dramatic cranial features. These prehistoric herbivores first emerged approximately 58 million years ago, less than 10 million years after an asteroid wiped out the dinosaurs, and persisted for roughly 20 million years.

This long-standing belief was recently challenged by research from the University of Kansas published in PLOS One. The discovery began when researchers tried to identify a broken upper molar fossil recovered during fieldwork in Montana. While examining nearly meter-long skulls housed at the American Museum of Natural History in New York to compare teeth, researcher Kevin Mulcahy noticed that physical variations across the skulls did not divide into two neat, binary categories.

What Scientists Found

To investigate the skull features further, Mulcahy expanded his dataset to include measurements from approximately 30 skulls belonging to a single extinct species, Uintatherium anceps. Statistical testing failed to show a clear division separating specimens into distinct larger and smaller groups. Instead of strong physical differences between males and females, the signals were faint and inconclusive.

To verify that the statistical methods were capable of detecting sexual dimorphism in fossilized species, the researchers tested the same techniques on modern bison. Bison were chosen because they are also large, horned plant-eaters with similar proposed ecological roles, but their sexes are known. The analysis clearly separated male and female bison, confirming that the weak signals found in uintatheres were due to actual skull morphology rather than limitations in the testing techniques.

Why It Matters

The study indicates that physical differences between male and female uintatheres were considerably less pronounced than paleontologists assumed for over a century. Researchers suggest that historical cultural attitudes from the late 19th and early 20th centuries, when these species were initially described, may have heavily influenced how early scientists interpreted ancient fossils.
