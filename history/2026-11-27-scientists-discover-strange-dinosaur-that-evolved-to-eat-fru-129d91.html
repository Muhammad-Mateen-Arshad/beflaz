Scientists Discover Strange Dinosaur That Evolved To Eat Fruit

Published on November 27, 2026

The Discovery

A newly identified turkey-sized dinosaur named Infernogallus jamesi has revealed that some prehistoric animals evolved specialized traits to eat fruit. Found in the Hell Creek Formation—a fossil site spanning Montana, North Dakota, South Dakota, and Wyoming—the creature lived in the Cretaceous period alongside famous dinosaurs such as T. rex and Triceratops. Its name translates to "hell chicken," referencing the geological formation where its partial skeleton and well-preserved skull were unearthed.

The research team included scientists from Stony Brook University, the Royal Ontario Museum, and the North Dakota Geological Survey, with their findings published in the journal Nature Communications. The animal's nearly complete skull and partial skeleton are currently showcased at the Royal Ontario Museum.

What Scientists Found

Despite originating from meat-eating lineage, Infernogallus jamesi belonged to the caenagnathids, a group of feathered, toothless oviraptorosaurs. An adult weighed roughly 25 pounds (12 kilograms), making it one of the smallest known members of this group. It is also the first dinosaur ever discovered with physical features specifically adapted for eating fruit.

By conducting CT scans on the fossilized skull, researchers found that the bones forming the roof of the mouth curved downward. This orientation placed certain jaw muscles horizontally rather than vertically. Unlike the rigid vertical biting mechanism of dinosaurs like T. rex, Infernogallus was able to slide its beak back and forth in a chewing motion. This specialized jaw setup resembles that of modern fruit-eating vulturine parrots, allowing the dinosaur to chew soft food in its mouth and strip the flesh off stone fruits away from their hard pits.

Why It Matters

As flowering plants expanded across land environments during the Cretaceous, fruits became larger and more abundant. The specialized diet of Infernogallus shows how dinosaurs adapted to this changing plant landscape.

Key takeaways from this discovery include:

  • It provides the first anatomical proof of fruit-eating specialization in a dinosaur.
  • It suggests that dinosaurs helped disperse seeds, filling ecological roles similar to those played by modern birds, reptiles, and small mammals.
  • It shows that dinosaur diversity and dietary roles remained complex near the end of the Cretaceous, contributing to discussions about whether dinosaurs were in decline prior to their extinction.
