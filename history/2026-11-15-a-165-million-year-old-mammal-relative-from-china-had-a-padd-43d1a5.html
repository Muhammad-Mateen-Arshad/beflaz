A 165-million-year-old mammal relative from China had a paddle-like tail and preserved throat suggesting aquatic hunting and unusual swallowing

Published on November 15, 2026

The conventional image of early mammalian ancestors often portrays tiny, uniform creatures hiding in a world dominated by massive dinosaurs. However, an extraordinary fossil unearthed in Northern China is revising our understanding of how these early relatives lived and evolved during the Jurassic Period.

The Discovery

The newly identified specimen represents a close relative of modern mammals named Megacauda. Living between 168 and 163 million years ago, this creature belongs to a group called mammaliaforms. Weighing approximately 1 to 2 kilograms, it is currently recognized as the largest mammaliaform known from the Jurassic Period, reaching a size comparable to a modern platypus.

What Scientists Found

Unlike many of its terrestrial kin, Megacauda possessed physical adaptations suited for a semiaquatic life, alongside exceptionally rare fossilized throat tissue:

  • A broad, flattened tail that likely assisted movement through aquatic environments.
  • Hind legs that were shorter compared to its forelegs and feet.
  • Unusual teeth tailored specifically for slicing meat rather than grinding plant material.
  • Preserved soft tissues from its throat region, including part of the pharynx and surrounding skeletal structure.

Because soft tissue rarely survives over long periods, the preservation of the throat is particularly remarkable. In modern mammals, this anatomical structure is essential for drinking, eating, and nursing. Based on its anatomy, researchers infer that this semiaquatic predator hunted prey such as fish, salamanders, and invertebrates.

Why It Matters

According to findings published in the journal Nature and summarized by Nature Portfolio, the fossil reveals that sophisticated mammalian feeding mechanisms—including the structures required for sucking and swallowing—had already developed by the Middle Jurassic. This pushes the timeline for the evolution of these traits farther back than previously demonstrated.

Furthermore, the discovery demonstrates that ancient mammalian relatives were far more ecologically diverse than long assumed. Even while dinosaurs dominated Jurassic ecosystems, early mammalian ancestors were actively experimenting with different lifestyles, occupying varied habitats, and pioneering new ways of surviving.
