Scientists Discover 115-Million-Year-Old Dinosaur Blood in Brazil

Published on November 17, 2026

The Discovery

In 2008, paleontologists uncovered a fossilized leg bone at Mina Pedra Branca, near Santana do Cariri in northeastern Brazil. Found within the Romualdo Formation, the remains belonged to Aratasaurus museunacionali, a meat-eating dinosaur from an early branch of coelurosaurian theropods that lived during the Early Cretaceous. Researchers estimate the animal was around four years old, measured 3.1 meters in length, and weighed about 34 kilograms.

An international team of scientists led by Professor Kliti Grice of Curtin University in Australia examined the 115-million-year-old fossil. Their study, published in the Proceedings of the National Academy of Sciences, details the rare chemical and structural preservation of ancient blood components hidden within the bone.

What Scientists Found

Using mass spectrometry, electron microscopy, and Raman spectroscopy, researchers inspected microscopic vascular canals where blood vessels once passed through the skeleton. Inside these tiny spaces, they discovered oval-shaped structures that resemble fossilized red blood cells, measuring roughly 23 micrometers long and 19.5 micrometers wide. Although larger and rounder than red blood cells in living crocodilians and birds, fossilization processes may have altered their original shape.

The investigation confirmed that the bone contains iron, carbon, nitrogen, heme B, and a modified compound named 3,8-diethyl heme B. While surrounding fossilized microbial material showed chemical signatures from chlorophyll, the bone was dominated by heme-related compounds. Although the fossil lacks liquid blood or intact hemoglobin proteins, it preserves mineralized biological cells and molecular remnants.

Why It Matters

The long-term survival of these fragile traces relies on unique fossilization conditions. The dinosaur died in a low-oxygen marine environment with an ancient microbial mat, which slowed down organic decay. Furthermore, researchers found that a manganese oxide mineral called hausmannite closely associated with the structures helped stabilize and protect the organic components over tens of millions of years.

This discovery provides valuable insights for paleontologists:

  • It proves that chemical components of dinosaur blood can survive over 100 million years in exceptional circumstances.
  • It explains how specific mineral interactions assist in preserving soft biological material in the fossil record.
  • It provides a new method for researchers to investigate oxygen transport and bodily functions in extinct species.
