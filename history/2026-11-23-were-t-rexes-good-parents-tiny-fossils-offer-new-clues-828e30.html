Were T. rexes good parents? Tiny fossils offer new clues

Published on November 23, 2026

While a fully grown Tyrannosaurus rex was one of the largest predators on land, new research shows that it began its life as a tiny, vulnerable creature. Rare fossils discovered in Alberta and Saskatchewan are giving paleontologists a clearer look into the early days and upbringing of these prehistoric animals.

The Discovery

Dr. Nick Longrich, who was a senior lecturer in paleontology and evolutionary biology at the University of Bath during the study, spotted several small bones in museum collections. While these were initially thought to belong to a small dinosaur species, the porous texture of the bones revealed they actually belonged to young tyrannosaurs.

What Scientists Found

To inspect the interior of the fossils, researchers brought them to the Canadian Light Source at the University of Saskatchewan. Using synchrotron X-rays, the team examined internal structures, including:

  • Blood vessel structures
  • Growth patterns
  • Variations in bone density

These scans enabled the team to confirm that the specimens were hatched young rather than unhatched embryos. The findings, published in the journal Biology, revealed that a T. rex hatched at roughly the size of a kitten. (The exact geological age of these fossils is not specified in the source; please check the official notice.)

Why It Matters

This evidence presents a fresh perspective on how tyrannosaurs raised their young. Rather than closely guarding a small family, adult tyrannosaurs likely produced large clutches of 25 to 100 eggs in a single nest. The parents offered more care than modern reptiles like turtles or crocodiles, but far less than modern birds. Operating more like "free-range" parents, they relied on producing large numbers of offspring, knowing many hatchlings would be consumed by predators before ever reaching adulthood.
