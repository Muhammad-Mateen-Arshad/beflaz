New visitors center opens at ancient Jewish and Christian site in Judean foothills

Published on November 15, 2026

The Discovery

A new visitors center has opened at Horbat Burgin, an ancient archaeological site located inside Adullam–France Park within the Judean foothills. The location contains historical remains that illustrate Jewish life during the Roman era as well as the area's later Byzantine Christian history. The launch comes after years of archaeological excavation, restoration, and public infrastructure work led by the Israel Antiquities Authority, Keren Kayemeth LeIsrael–Jewish National Fund, and the Mateh Yehuda Regional Council.

What Scientists Found

Excavation director Dr. Amir Ganor noted that Horbat Burgin is the largest ancient settlement in the surrounding area in both physical footprint and surviving structural remains. Archaeological evidence indicates that distinct civilizations occupied the location over thousands of years.

Major historical elements at the complex include:

  • A subterranean network of rock-carved tunnels constructed roughly 2,000 years ago, which Jewish rebels utilized as a base of operations during the Bar Kokhba Revolt of 132–136 AD.
  • An ancient Byzantine church featuring vibrant, colorful mosaic flooring.
  • Remains of a Roman-era Jewish village that researchers believe was named Caphar Bish, a settlement documented in historical accounts by Flavius Josephus.

Findings show that Roman forces eventually discovered and demolished the rebel base, though another Jewish village was constructed over the ruins many years later. Opening hours and admission details were not specified in the source; please check the official notice.

Why It Matters

Before recent protection and restoration efforts, Horbat Burgin suffered heavy damage from ongoing antiquities looting. Looters repeatedly broke into surface buildings, burial caves, and subterranean hiding complexes. Intervention by inspectors and conservation teams successfully halted further destruction and preserved the site for the public.

The development creates a new educational destination for rural tourism, safeguarding historical heritage while allowing visitors to explore subterranean systems and ancient architecture firsthand.
