Prehistoric Humans May Have Hunted Hippos With Poisoned Spears

Published on November 22, 2026

The Discovery

Deep within the desert of southern Saudi Arabia's Najran Province, ancient seashells reveal a dramatically different past. Approximately 125,000 years ago, this area housed a 130-square-mile freshwater body known as Lake Mundafan, which reached depths of around 100 feet. A study published in Science details how an international team of archaeologists excavated the site, unveiling how early human populations utilized the abundant resources of this prehistoric wetland.

What Scientists Found

Excavations produced over 5,500 stone artifacts along with various animal remains, including more than 100 ostrich eggshell fragments and over 130 fossilized bones from creatures like hippopotamuses, antelopes, and gazelles. Although the animal bones did not show explicit butchery marks, their high concentration right next to stone implements suggests they were hunted for food.

Microscopic analysis of the stone implements uncovered preserved organic matter. Tools displayed traces of cattails, cucumber relatives, and palm leaf fibers. Researchers concluded that lakeside groups did not merely consume these plants, but worked them into practical items including cordage, matting, and bindings.

Why It Matters

The findings illustrate that prehistoric humans possessed surprisingly sophisticated Stone Age technology. Analysis of stone spear tips revealed plant adhesives on the hafts and plant-derived toxins on the points. The hunters harvested cardiac glycosides from the arrow poison tree—a substance that lowers heart rates—giving them a deadly advantage when targeting giant herbivores like hippos. Rather than acting as a simple watering hole, the ancient lake served as a central focal point for complex technological, crafting, and hunting activities.
