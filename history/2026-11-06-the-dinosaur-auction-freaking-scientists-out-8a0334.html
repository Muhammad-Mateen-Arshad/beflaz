The Dinosaur Auction Freaking Scientists Out

Published on November 06, 2026

The scientific community is locked in a intense debate over the scheduled Paris auction of Dubreuillosaurus valesdunensis, a rare fossil set to go under the hammer at the Millon auction house on November 18.

The Discovery

The story began in 1994 when André Dubreuil, then the mayor of Conteville in Normandy, France, noticed dinosaur bone fragments and teeth while examining a quarry on his land. In 1998, a team of researchers from the National Museum of Natural History in Paris arrived to explore the site. Over four digging seasons between 1999 and 2001, the team collected more than 2,000 fossil fragments, unearthing one of the most remarkably preserved megalosaur skull pieces ever found.

What Scientists Found

After spending two years painstakingly reconstructing the skull fragments, paleontologist Ronan Allain formally named the species Dubreuillosaurus valesdunensis in a 2005 paper. Research showed that the fossil belonged to a 167-million-year-old juvenile carnivorous theropod that measured about nine feet long. A relative of Megalosaurus, the creature possessed a long snout that it likely used to catch fish.

Why It Matters

This fossil is a holotype, meaning it is the singular reference specimen used by scientists to define the species and categorize future finds. Because it is the only known specimen of its kind, the planned sale has raised severe concerns among paleontologists.

  • Scientific Concerns: Experts including Steve Brusatte, Kristina Curry Rogers, and Marie-Béatrice Forel warn that auctioning a unique holotype sets a dangerous precedent, treating irreplaceable scientific evidence as a private financial asset.
  • Campaigning Against the Sale: Forel, along with colleagues and the French Paleontological Association, has launched petitions, social media initiatives, and fundraising drives to try to secure the fossil for public scientific research.
  • The Auctioneer's Stance: Representatives from the Millon auction house argue that private buyers frequently assist public science by preserving and lending fossils to museums, noting that this specimen has been privately owned and loaned to institutions for nearly 30 years.
