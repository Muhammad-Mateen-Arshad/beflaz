Ancient pottery reveals a 6,000-year-old brewing tradition in Shanxi

Published on November 07, 2026

Archaeologists from the Shanxi Provincial Institute of Archaeology and Stanford University have uncovered ancient alcohol residues on pottery excavated from the Xinghuacun archaeological site in Fenyang, Shanxi province. The findings confirm that alcohol production was active in the region between 6,000 and 5,500 years ago, during the middle Yangshao period.

The Discovery

The analyzed ceramics were originally excavated in 2022 from the floors of residential houses located near Fenghaogou village. In May 2025, experts gathered residue samples from the artifacts kept at the Fenyang City Museum. Laboratory analyses were subsequently completed at both the Stanford University Archaeology Center and the Chinese Academy of Social Sciences workstation laboratory in Luoyang.

What Scientists Found

Using microfossil residue analysis technology, researchers examined ten distinct ceramic items, which included:

  • Two narrow-mouthed bottles with pointed bottoms
  • Two painted bowls
  • Six pottery jars

Testing revealed microscopic plant structures called phytoliths, fungal spores, and starch grains showing clear signs of processing, such as gelatinization. This structural damage demonstrates that the pointed-bottom vessels and certain jars were used for brewing or storing alcohol. The study confirmed that local crops were used in the process and identified an unbroken lineage of brewing-related microorganisms. Because these vessels were found inside ordinary residential homes, researchers concluded that alcohol consumption was a common practice across the community, where feasting served as a clan ritual.

Why It Matters

The discovery fills a key gap in the prehistoric brewing archaeology of the Yellow River basin. Institute leadership noted that the research provides essential support for tracing the continuous development of early Chinese civilization, showing how alcohol helped shape community exchanges and ritual structures. The local liquor-making tradition, celebrated in poetry as far back as the Tang Dynasty, continues into modern times as Xinghuacun remains a major hub for light aroma baijiu distillation.
