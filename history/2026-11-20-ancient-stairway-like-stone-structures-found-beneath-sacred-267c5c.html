Ancient Stairway-Like Stone Structures Found Beneath Sacred Lake Titicaca

Published on November 20, 2026

The Discovery

During a dive in the first week of November near the Island of the Sun, members of the Bolivian Navy’s Sport Diving Club spotted striking underwater rock formations in Lake Titicaca. The arrangement of the stones resembles a staircase or an old wall, suggesting the possibility of ancient human construction. However, no formal archaeological examination or dating has been conducted yet. Captain Max Álvaro Villarroel Muñoz of the Bolivian Navy noted that scientific research is necessary to determine whether these shapes are man-made ruins or natural rock alignments.

What Scientists Found

Lake Titicaca has hosted underwater archaeological investigations for many years, yielding vast evidence of ancient civilizations:

  • More than 1,350 dives starting in 2012 led to the identification of roughly 20 underwater archaeological sites and over 20,000 artifacts spanning from the Tiwanaku era to the Inca Empire.
  • At Khoa Reef, researchers documented Tiwanaku religious offerings, including gold ornaments, ceramic incense burners, shell objects, and bones of sacrificed young llamas.
  • In 2014, a preserved stone box was discovered six meters deep at K’akaya Reef, containing a miniature llama or alpaca figure carved from a seashell and a rolled gold sheet left by Inca worshippers.
  • Studies on the Island of the Sun trace human activity back to at least 3500 BC at Ch’uxuqullu, where stone tools and obsidian from the Chivay region reveal early boat navigation and mainland trade networks.

Why It Matters

These underwater and coastal discoveries illustrate how Lake Titicaca served as a significant ceremonial hub and trade network for successive Andean cultures. Rituals performed in the lake reinforced political and religious power during the Tiwanaku era, while later Inca society continued the sacred tradition of submerged offerings. Although archaeologists have not yet linked the newly found stone formations to previously documented ceremonial sites, the discovery opens up new potential for understanding the deep history of South America's sacred lake.
