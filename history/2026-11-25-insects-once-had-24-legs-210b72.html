Insects once had 24 legs

Published on November 25, 2026

The Discovery

Modern insects are defined by their six-legged bodies, but a prehistoric fossil reveals that this was not always the case. Collected in 1985 from a rocky area near Big Bend National Park in southwestern Texas, a 324-million-year-old fossil was initially identified as a juvenile crustacean named Tesnusocaris goldichi. Deemed unremarkable at the time, it spent decades stored away in a San Diego museum drawer.

The specimen got a second look when Erik Tihelka, a paleoentomologist at the University of Cambridge, noticed an illustration of it in a secondhand Russian paleontology book. Curious about its classification, Tihelka visited the San Diego Museum of Natural History to inspect the fossil. By examining the dark specimen under polarized light filters to reduce reflection, he uncovered structural details that proved it was actually an early relative of modern insects.

What Scientists Found

The research team named the fossil Chosha praecursor, drawing from a Navajo creation myth and combining the Navajo word for insect with the Latin word for predecessor. Analysis of the fossil revealed a unique anatomy:

  • A thorax split into three segments and an abdomen divided into 11 sections.
  • A single whiplike antenna, a stiff tube designed for laying eggs, and a long central tail flanked by two shorter ones.
  • Three main pairs of walking legs along with nine additional pairs of shorter legs along its abdomen, totaling 24 legs.

Because its rear legs were shaped like paddles, researchers believe Chosha praecursor led a semi-aquatic lifestyle, using its extra limbs to paddle or scuttle through shallow, muddy waters.

Why It Matters

The findings, published on August 26 in the journal Nature, help clear up a long-standing mystery in insect evolution. By comparing Chosha with similar ancient specimens found in Scotland and Illinois, Tihelka placed these creatures on a family tree positioned just outside living insects. Experts like Dany Azar and William Shear note that the fossil fills a crucial gap in the sparse fossil record between 325 and 400 million years ago. It demonstrates how multi-legged, water-dwelling ancestors evolved over generations, eventually losing extra limbs to become the six-legged terrestrial insects seen today.
