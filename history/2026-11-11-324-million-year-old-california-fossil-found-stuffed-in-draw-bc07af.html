324-million-year old California fossil found stuffed in drawer changes everything we know about life on Earth

Published on November 11, 2026

For four decades, a 324-million-year-old fossil sat in a basement drawer at the San Diego Natural History Museum. Cataloged as specimen No. 28852, the artifact was originally retrieved during a 1985 dig in Brewster County, Texas, and was categorized as an ancient crustacean in 1991. However, a fresh examination has completely rewritten its role in the history of life on Earth.

The Discovery

The breakthrough occurred after Erik Tihelka, a doctoral researcher at Cambridge University, spotted photos of the forgotten fossil in a textbook. He flew to Southern California to examine the slab in person and realized scientists had misidentified it. The museum has since reclassified the artifact as an insect named Chosha praecursor.

What Scientists Found

The physical structure of the fossil features a blend of characteristics from different groups:

  • Classic insect traits, including six legs joined to its thorax, an egg-laying organ, and a tail-like filament.
  • Crustacean traits, including several additional pairs of paddle-like legs extending from its abdomen.

Researchers suggest the creature used its extra abdominal paddles to glide through aquatic environments, offering a clear view of how water-dwelling ancestors evolved into modern insects that walk and fly.

Why It Matters

The fossil is now considered one of the earliest uncontested insects ever identified. Its reclassification provides critical evidence during the "hexapod gap," a mysterious 60-million-year period in the fossil record where early bugs appeared to disappear. Museum leaders have brought the evolutionary relic out of storage and secured it inside a glass display case on the museum's lower level for visitors to view.
