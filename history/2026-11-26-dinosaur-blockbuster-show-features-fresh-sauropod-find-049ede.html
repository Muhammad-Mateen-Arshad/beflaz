Dinosaur blockbuster show features fresh sauropod find

Published on November 26, 2026

The Discovery

Paleontologists excavating a remote desert site in Patagonia have uncovered the remains of what is believed to be a newly identified dinosaur species. The breakthrough began when researchers spotted a single fossilized vertebra lying on the rocky surface of the terrain. Led by Dr. Matthew McCurry from the Australian Museum alongside a team of Argentinian scientists, the group dug several meters into a hillside using tools ranging from brushes and chisels to hammers and jackhammers.

What Scientists Found

Unlike many excavations that yield only isolated fragments, the team uncovered a substantial section of the animal's skeleton. Their findings include:

  • Multiple additional vertebrae
  • Two limbs
  • A tail

Estimated to be between 110 million and 115 million years old, the bones differ from previous discoveries in the region. Researchers believe the creature is an early relative of the titanosaurs, a group within the sauropod family. Sauropods were plant-eating dinosaurs known for their small heads, pillar-like legs, and exceptionally long necks and tails.

Why It Matters

Patagonia is a major hub for paleontology because persistent winds gradually erode surface rock, revealing long-buried fossils. The newly found fossils were carefully packed to be flown to Australia for an exhibition titled The World's Biggest Dinosaur: Dinosaurs of Patagonia at the Australian Museum. Developed by Argentina's Museum of Paleontology Egidio Feruglio, the show displays 16 full-size casts representing 13 species, including a 38-meter-long replica of Patagotitan mayorum, a massive 70-tonne creature considered the largest animal ever to walk the planet.

Though runway repairs temporarily delayed the fossils' flight to Sydney ahead of the event's opening, field work in South America has also hit a temporary pause. The research team reburied the dig site to protect the remaining fossils from winter cold and frost damage, while also seeking funding for future work. Dr. McCurry plans to return to the location in 2027 to continue excavating the rest of the skeleton.
