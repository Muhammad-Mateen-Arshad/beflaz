Scientists discover a giant 66-pound rodent that lived 34 million years ago

Published on November 16, 2026

The Discovery

An international team of paleontologists has identified an ancient rodent of unusual size from fossils uncovered in southern Thailand. The discovery was made at the Bang Mark Pit inside the Krabi lignite mine, located in Thailand's Krabi Province. The research effort involves scientists from McGill University, Thailand's Department of Mineral Resources, and French institutions including CNRS and Université de Poitiers.

What Scientists Found

The newly classified species, named Yakamys sirindhornae, lived approximately 34 million years ago during the middle of the Paleogene period. The find consists of just two fossilized teeth: a lower second molar and a lower incisor. Despite the limited physical evidence, these specimens offer key details about early animal evolution in the region.

The name given to the prehistoric creature highlights both its physical nature and its background:

  • Yakamys: Merges the Thai word "yak" (meaning giant) with the Greek word "mys" (meaning mouse or rat).
  • sirindhornae: Honors Princess Maha Chakri Sirindhorn of Thailand for her continuous support of scientific and paleontological research.

Estimating Body Size

To determine how large Yakamys sirindhornae was, researchers analyzed the tooth dimensions using seven established body-mass calculation methods. Their calculations indicate the animal weighed roughly 30 kilograms, making it about double the weight of contemporary rodents from the same era, such as Hulgana. However, because body size calculations based on a few teeth carry noticeable uncertainty, scientists emphasize that this figure serves as a general estimate.

Why It Matters

While today's largest living rodent is the capybara, giant rodents evolved at various points in earth's history, most notably in South America. The presence of Yakamys sirindhornae proves that rodent gigantism developed much earlier and across a wider geographic area than previously understood, establishing Southeast Asia as an important setting for early large-bodied rodent evolution. Researchers note that further fossil discoveries will be required to clarify the physical traits, lifestyle, and precise evolutionary tree of this ancient species.
