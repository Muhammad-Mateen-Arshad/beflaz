We May Have Found Dinosaur Blood in a Fossil, But That Doesn't Mean We're Close to Bringing Dinosaurs Back

Published on November 19, 2026

The Discovery

In previous research described in Nature Communications, scientists examined an ancient tick fossilized in amber while holding a dinosaur feather. Known informally as "Dracula's terrible tick," the specimen contained blood, but mineral deposits prevented researchers from analyzing its contents. A newer study published in the Proceedings of the National Academy of Sciences has provided a different path forward by analyzing dinosaur bone rather than preserved insects.

This recent study focused on exceptionally preserved hind limb bones from Aratasaurus museunacionali, a theropod dinosaur that lived in modern-day Brazil approximately 115 million years ago.

What Scientists Found

Researchers identified heme signatures located in mineralized red blood cells inside the vascular canals of the fossilized bone. This discovery demonstrates that original biological molecules can persist inside fossils across tens of millions of years.

However, experts clarify that this finding does not mean scientists will uncover fluid dinosaur blood, viable DNA, or undamaged cells. Fossilization fundamentally alters the chemical composition and physical structure of cells, even when a recognizable molecular fingerprint survives.

Why It Matters

Although finding intact blood remains out of reach, detecting preserved molecular signals grants paleontologists new tools to investigate prehistoric biology. Potential areas of future research include:

  • Comparing molecular details with modern species like birds and crocodilians.
  • Examining how factors such as growth, physical injury, disease, and stress influenced ancient blood chemistry.
  • Uncovering the mineral and chemical pathways that enable delicate biological information to survive for 115 million years.

By understanding how these biological fingerprints endure, scientists can apply advanced chemical and imaging methods to inspect other fossils far more effectively.
