A new fossil from South Africa offers clues to how giant plant‑eating dinosaurs evolved

Published on November 26, 2026

The Discovery

South Africa’s fossil-rich landscape plays a central role in untangling how prehistoric reptiles evolved into massive herbivores. Vertebrate paleontologists have described a new dinosaur species named Nyana rooiwalensis, which lived during the Late Triassic period roughly 210 million years ago. The fossil was recovered from the lower layers of the Elliot Formation, a rock sequence formed by floodwaters between 215 million and 185 million years ago.

Discovered near the village of Qhemegha in the Eastern Cape province, close to the border of Lesotho, the site in Rooiwal gave the species its specific name. Affectionately nicknamed "Big Baby," the name Nyana draws from local languages, meaning "son" or child in isiXhosa and "small" in Sesotho, reflecting the animal's physical size relative to its young age.

What Scientists Found

The centerpiece of the discovery is an exceptionally preserved, near-complete skull. It represents the highest quality dinosaur skull ever recovered from the Late Triassic sections of the Elliot Formation, where earlier digs had only uncovered fragmentary skull remains from species such as Kholumolumo. The specimen preserves several critical features, including:

  • The braincase and palate
  • Bones connected to the eye, throat, and tongue

Researchers examined thin slices of the dinosaur's bones under a microscope—a method called osteohistology—to study its growth. The bone tissue revealed a maximum of two seasonal growth lines. This indicates that while the individual had already reached a weight of approximately 400 kilograms, it was still at a very early stage of life when it died.

Why It Matters

Millions of years after Nyana lived, sauropods like Brachiosaurus and Diplodocus emerged around 150 million years ago, reaching up to 40 meters in length and weighing 80 tonnes—equal to about 40 African elephants. The remains of Nyana rooiwalensis show that high growth rates and substantial body size were already developing early in sauropodomorph history, offering vital clues into how these creatures eventually evolved into the largest land animals on Earth.
