Tiny reptile found in Wyoming sandstone is a prehistoric ‘dragon’

Published on November 22, 2026

The Discovery

In August 2022, an excavation team at the Meilyn Quarry in Wyoming uncovered a slab of sandstone containing tiny vertebrae from an unidentified creature buried roughly 156 million years ago. In January 2023, a materials scientist from the Paul Scherrer Institute (PSI) in Switzerland visited the Aathal Dinosaur Museum and offered to help analyze hard-to-identify specimens using advanced imaging. Shortly after, the museum provided the mystery sandstone block for investigation.

To look inside the rock without damaging it, a research team from PSI, the University of Zurich, Empa, and the Aathal Dinosaur Museum combined X-ray scans with neutron imaging. Because neutrons are sensitive to hydrogen present in bone minerals, researchers could separate the hidden skeleton from the surrounding sandstone across tens of thousands of cross-sectional images.

What Scientists Found

The digital reconstruction revealed a 45-centimeter-long reptile featuring two horn-like spikes on the back of its skull. Its head was found detached from its body, leading paleontologists to suspect a larger predator may have severed the torso before fine river sand buried the remains.

Despite the separation, the specimen yielded pelvic bones, part of a hind leg, and an almost intact braincase measuring less than two centimeters across. Researchers named the newly identified species Meilynodraco amonamarthi, combining references to the quarry, the head horns, and the Swedish metal band Amon Amarth.

Why It Matters

The fossil is the oldest choristodere known from North America and the most complete early member of this extinct group ever discovered. Paleontologists have long debated how choristoderes relate to other reptiles due to their unusual skull shapes. Key findings from the study include:

  • Inner ear bones that bear a striking similarity to those of living crown-group reptiles.
  • New evidence suggesting choristoderes may be more closely related to modern reptiles than earlier fossil discoveries indicated.

The team published their findings in the journal Royal Society Open Science. The fossil is scheduled to go on public display in early 2027 as part of a traveling exhibition at the Aathal Dinosaur Museum.
