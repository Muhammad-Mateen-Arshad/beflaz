Scientists say they've found the strongest evidence yet of red blood cells in a dinosaur bone

Published on November 07, 2026

The Discovery

Researchers studying a 115 million-year-old fossilized dinosaur bone have uncovered what may be the strongest evidence yet of preserved red blood cells in an ancient specimen. The leg bone belonged to Aratasaurus museunacionali, a theropod dinosaur from the Early Cretaceous period. It was excavated in 2008 at the Mina Pedra Branca geological site in northeastern Brazil, located within the Romualdo Formation. Scientists believe that stagnant, oxygen-deprived waters at the location limited decomposition and helped safeguard fragile organic materials over millions of years.

What Scientists Found

Because soft tissues and cells rarely survive fossilization, reports of ancient biological preservation frequently spark controversy among scientists. In a study published in the journal Proceedings of the National Academy of Sciences, researchers analyzed tiny channels in the bone known as vascular canals. Inside these structures, they identified an abundance of oval-shaped microstructures resembling red blood cells.

Using microscopic imaging alongside chemical analyses, the research team detected heme—the oxygen-binding component of hemoglobin—directly inside the structures. This represents the first time heme has been identified in situ within a dinosaur fossil. Scientists also found traces of manganese oxides inside the bone, which likely helped stabilize and preserve the microstructures.

Why It Matters

According to lead author Kliti Grice of Curtin University, finding cellular and molecular remains offers direct access to the biological chemistry and physiology of extinct creatures rather than just their anatomy. Applying these combined imaging and chemical techniques to museum collections worldwide could reveal preserved biological information that was previously overlooked.

However, scientists urge caution before drawing definitive conclusions:

  • Not all paleontology experts are convinced that the microscopic shapes are genuine red blood cells.
  • The microstructures differ in size and shape from red blood cells observed in modern crocodilians and birds.
  • Physical scientist Timothy Cleland noted that finding heme does not imply dinosaur DNA has survived, as the two molecules preserve in entirely different ways.

Researchers plan to examine additional fossils from various time periods and environments to determine how common this level of preservation might be.
