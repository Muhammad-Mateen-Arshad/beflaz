Archaeologists at This Ancient Maya Metropolis in the Forest of Belize Are Debunking Long-Held Myths About the Mesoamerican Civilization

Published on October 26, 2026

The Discovery

Deep within the Chiquibul rainforest of Belize lies Caracol, an ancient Maya metropolis that was long concealed by dense tropical jungle. Unlike other Maya strongholds such as Tikal and Chichén Itzá, which European colonists encountered in the nineteenth century, Caracol remained largely unexcavated until archaeologists Arlen and Diane Chase arrived in the 1980s. Working alongside researchers including their son Adrian Chase, the team conducts fieldwork from a remote camp of zinc-roofed shacks featuring no running water, no cellphone service, and fickle electricity.

What Scientists Found

For centuries, popular assumptions about Maya civilization were shaped by colonial-era accounts, notably those of sixteenth-century Spanish friar Diego de Landa, who burned vast quantities of Maya hieroglyphic codices. Spanish writers projected their own worldview onto the region, framing Maya society as a simple two-class system of ruling elites and scattered peasants. Later twentieth-century scholars acknowledged Maya achievements in calendar design, astronomy, and architecture, but still categorized the Maya as peaceful stargazers who lived in near-empty ceremonial cities.

Rigorous field excavations by the Chase team effectively dismantled these myths. While researching at Santa Rita Corozal, the archaeologists found imported obsidian tools in non-elite households, proving that ordinary residents had access to regional trade markets for goods such as jade, ceramics, and volcanic stone. Later research at Caracol confirmed that the remote site was far from an empty religious husk, revealing a bustling urban center with a peak population exceeding 100,000 residents.

Why It Matters

These findings have transformed our understanding of ancient Mesoamerican history by correcting centuries of misinformation:

  • Disproved colonial assumptions that forced a rigid, two-class feudal structure onto Maya society.
  • Overturned long-standing theories that Maya cities were unpopulated ceremonial centers.
  • Demonstrated that non-elite households actively participated in open trade networks to obtain valuable imported materials.
