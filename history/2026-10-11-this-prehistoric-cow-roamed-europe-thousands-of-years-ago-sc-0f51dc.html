This prehistoric cow roamed Europe thousands of years ago. Scientists now know why it perished

Published on October 11, 2026

The Discovery

The aurochs was a massive prehistoric animal that once ranged across Europe, Asia, and northern Africa. While global populations eventually vanished in the 17th century when the final confirmed individual died in Poland, the species disappeared from Scandinavia much earlier. An international research team led by Sweden's Lund University set out to determine why this regional collapse occurred, publishing their findings in the journal The Holocene.

To trace the history of the species, researchers analyzed ancient aurochs bones recovered from bogs and historical settlements across Denmark and southern Sweden. By matching the ages and counts of these remains against archaeological and environmental records, the team reconstructed how the animals responded to changing landscapes and human pressures over thousands of years.

What Scientists Found

The study revealed that the downfall of Scandinavian aurochs was driven by multiple overlapping stressors rather than a single cause:

  • Genetic Bottlenecks: Around 11,700 years ago, aurochs moved northward across a land corridor created by low sea levels. Moving farther north reduced their genetic diversity, creating an evolutionary bottleneck across regions like Jutland, Zealand, and Scania.
  • Geographical Isolation: Genetic evidence showed that Scandinavian aurochs maintained contact with mainland European populations for 2,000 years longer than previously assumed, until rising water levels flooded the connecting land bridge and isolated the population.
  • Human Hunting and Habitat Loss: Intense hunting by Mesolithic groups coincided with this isolation. Later, as open landscapes transformed into dense forests in southern Sweden, the remaining low-density populations were pushed to extinction.

These combined pressures caused the species to disappear from the island of Zealand around 8,500 years ago, while small numbers persisted in southern Sweden until at least 6,500 years ago before perishing completely.

Why It Matters

Understanding how prehistoric megafauna succumbed to a combination of environmental changes, habitat isolation, and human hunting offers crucial lessons for modern wildlife management. The researchers highlight that species rarely vanish due to a single issue. Consequently, current conservation plans must address multiple threats at once and focus on creating connected ecological corridors rather than relying solely on isolated nature reserves.
