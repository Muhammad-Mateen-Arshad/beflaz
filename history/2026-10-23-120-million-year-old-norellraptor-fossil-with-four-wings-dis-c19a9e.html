120-million-year-old Norellraptor fossil with four wings discovered in the Jiufotang Formation, Liaoning, China

Published on October 23, 2026

The Discovery

An international team of researchers has uncovered a remarkably preserved fossil of a four-winged flying dinosaur known as Norellraptor barsboldi. Found in the Jiufotang Formation in Liaoning, northeastern China, the fossil is 120 million years old. Thanks to the unique geological conditions of the location, soft tissues that usually disappear over time were kept intact, enabling scientists to study detailed feather impressions preserved in the rock. Analysis of the specimen revealed that the animal was approximately three years old when it died.

What Scientists Found

Reaching a length of only 57 centimeters, Norellraptor is classified within the microraptorines, a group of small dinosaurs related to modern birds. Its most surprising feature is the presence of long feathers attached to both its arms and legs, giving it an appearance similar to a four-winged animal or dragon. Examination of its teeth indicates that it was an agile, opportunistic hunter. These traits suggest that the dinosaur could perform complex aerial maneuvers to effectively capture small prey.

Why It Matters

As detailed in a study published in the journal Nature Communications, this discovery reshapes the scientific understanding of how flight originated. Statistical analyses show that changes in the creature's bone structure occurred in a different sequence than in current birds. This indicates that the evolution of flight was a complex and varied process involving multiple evolutionary experiments, rather than a single shared pathway. Flight capabilities likely developed independently across distinct dinosaur branches in response to common environmental pressures.

Future Research

Scientists are now focused on building digital simulations to model how Norellraptor navigated its natural environment. Early findings indicate that it combined all four limbs to execute advanced gliding techniques. By digitally replicating its movement, experts hope to gain clearer insights into its flight mechanics, hunting capabilities, and ecological interactions.
