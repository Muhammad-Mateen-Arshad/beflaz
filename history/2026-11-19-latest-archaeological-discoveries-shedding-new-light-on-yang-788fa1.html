Latest archaeological discoveries shedding new light on Yangtze River civilization

Published on November 19, 2026

Recent progress under the "Archaeology China" program has offered significant insights into the development of human civilization across the Yangtze River basin. Announced by China's National Cultural Heritage Administration at a meeting in Beijing on Wednesday, the findings encompass research from four major excavation projects.

The Discovery

Archaeologists conducted research at four distinct locations: the Taohuahe site in Suining, Sichuan Province; the Fanshan site in Hangzhou, Zhejiang Province; the Lingtoushang site in Jiujiang, Jiangxi Province; and the Laohudun cemetery site in Yunmeng County, Hubei Province. Together, these sites chart human activity, settlement patterns, and political evolution in the region across thousands of years.

What Scientists Found

Excavations across the four locations revealed a wide array of architectural layouts and ancient tools:

  • Taohuahe site: Researchers uncovered pebble-paved activity areas and a standardized, distinctive large-flake chopper tool industry.
  • Fanshan site: Associated with the Neolithic Liangzhu culture from around 5,000 years ago, this location revealed a layout integrating burial grounds, ceremonial areas, and surrounding enclosure structures. Lead archaeologist Wang Ningyuan noted that these ceremonial spaces were directly tied to the primary axis of the water management network, showing how social power, ritual practices, and water control projects were interconnected.
  • Lingtoushang site: Excavators uncovered settlements dating from the Neolithic era to the Shang Dynasty (around 1600–1046 BC).
  • Laohudun cemetery: Findings included inscribed bamboo and wooden slips alongside numerous valuable lacquered wooden objects. Exact itemized counts are not specified in the source; please check the official notice.

Why It Matters

These discoveries significantly improve the understanding of how early societies evolved in the Yangtze River basin. The discoveries at Taohuahe demonstrate early spatial planning and structured campsite organization. The Fanshan site provides material evidence of royal institutional authority and ritual practices in Liangzhu culture. Furthermore, Lingtoushang illustrates early cultural exchanges and resource control linking the Central Plains with the middle reaches of the Yangtze. Finally, the written slips and artifacts at Laohudun supply primary evidence for analyzing grassroots social governance and state expansion during the unified Qin and Han dynasties (221 BC–AD 220).
