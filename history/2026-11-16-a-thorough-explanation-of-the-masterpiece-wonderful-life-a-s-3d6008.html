A Thorough Explanation of the Masterpiece "Wonderful Life"! A Scientific Mystery About Strange 500-Million-Year-Old Fossils and the Contingency of Evolution

Published on November 16, 2026

The Discovery

In 1989, evolutionary biologist Stephen Jay Gould published a non-fiction book titled "Wonderful Life: The Burgess Shale and the Nature of History." Spanning over 500 pages, the work highlights extraordinary ocean fossils unearthed from Canada's Burgess Shale, a geological layer dating back roughly 500 million years to the Cambrian period. Unlike most fossils that preserve only hard bones and shells, this site remarkably preserved soft body tissue, revealing marine creatures that were widely unknown to the general public.

What Scientists Found

The fossils revealed a vast array of ancient sea life with body structures completely different from modern organisms. Some notable examples include:

  • Opabinia: A strange animal featuring five eyes on its head and a long, hose-like proboscis extending from its front.
  • Anomalocaris: A large top predator of ancient seas. Researchers initially believed its distinct body parts were separate animals—resembling jellyfish and shrimp—before discovering they all belonged to one giant creature.
  • Pikaia: A plain, small creature similar to a lancelet, which represents an important relative in the lineage leading to modern vertebrates, including humans.

Why It Matters

Gould used these findings to counter the conventional belief that evolution is a simple, step-by-step ladder progressing from basic organisms to superior beings. Instead, he argued that structural diversity among living things actually reached its peak during the early Cambrian period. Many unique basic designs later vanished not because the organisms were weak or inferior, but simply due to chance. Because survival was heavily influenced by luck, Gould concluded that if the tape of Earth's history were rewound and replayed from the start, humans might never have evolved.
