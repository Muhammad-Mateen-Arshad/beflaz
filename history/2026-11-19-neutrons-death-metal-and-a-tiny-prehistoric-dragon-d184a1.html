Neutrons, death metal and a tiny prehistoric dragon

Published on November 19, 2026

The Discovery

In August 2022, an excavation team from the Aathal Dinosaur Museum uncovered a tiny fossilized skeleton encased in sandstone at Meilyn Quarry in Wyoming, USA. Initially, researchers were uncertain whether the partial remains belonged to a baby dinosaur, a prehistoric snake, or an early relative of crocodiles. The specimen was brought to Switzerland, where Pavel Trtik, a materials scientist at the Paul Scherrer Institute (PSI), collaborated with palaeontologist Torsten Scheyer from the University of Zurich, museum director Yolanda Schicker-Siber, and two undergraduate students from Worcester Polytechnic Institute to identify the creature.

What Scientists Found

To view the bones hidden inside the sandstone, the international team combined neutron imaging with X-ray tomography carried out at PSI, Empa in Dübendorf, and the University of Zurich. Neutrons are especially sensitive to the hydrogen inside bone minerals, allowing researchers to distinguish the skeleton from the rock. By analyzing tens of thousands of cross-sectional scans, they uncovered pelvic bones, portions of a hind leg, and a skull with a remarkably preserved braincase measuring under two centimeters wide.

The reptile lived roughly 156 million years ago during the Jurassic period, grew to about 45 centimeters in length, and possessed two dragon-like horns on the back of its head. Because the skull was detached from the torso, scientists believe the animal may have been killed or scavenged by a larger predator in an ancient river before being covered in sand.

Why It Matters

The fossil represents a brand-new species named Meilynodraco amonamarthi by Scheyer, who was inspired by hard rock music and the works of J. R. R. Tolkien. The reptile belongs to choristoderes, a group of extinct reptiles whose evolutionary history has long puzzled scientists. Tiny inner ear bones in the fossil closely resemble those of modern living reptiles, indicating that choristoderes may be more closely related to today's reptiles than earlier evidence suggested. The find marks the oldest recorded choristodere in North America and the most complete early-branching member of the group discovered so far.
