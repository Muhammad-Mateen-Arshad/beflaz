A dinosaur found by a Montana rancher on her land in 2019 sold for $47.3 million, over five times its top estimate

Published on November 22, 2026

A remarkably preserved prehistoric skeleton has become a central point in both paleontology and the commercial fossil market, raising key questions about scientific access to ancient discoveries.

The Discovery

In 2019, landowner Jodi Pierson discovered a dinosaur skeleton on her private property near Jordan, Montana. A seven-person team, including Pierson and Clayton Phipps, excavated the specimen during a single field season. Originating from the Hell Creek Formation and dated to approximately 67 million years ago, the skeleton was provisionally identified as Nanotyrannus cf. lancensis.

The fossil comprises roughly 191 bone elements from one individual. It includes a nearly complete skull with 53 bones and around 50 teeth, an intact wishbone, and about half of its belly ribs. The mounted skeleton measures approximately 19.6 feet long and 9.6 feet tall, with missing elements restored using 3D printing and sculpting.

What Scientists Found

For decades, researchers debated whether Nanotyrannus was its own distinct species or merely a juvenile Tyrannosaurus rex. In fact, this specific skeleton was displayed as a young T. rex at an art fair in 2023. However, studies published in late 2025 in Science and Nature demonstrated that specimens classified as Nanotyrannus were mature adults, possessing unique traits such as larger forelimbs, more teeth, and fewer tail vertebrae than T. rex. Paleontologist Lindsay Zanno noted that these findings fundamentally altered longstanding assumptions in tyrannosaur research.

Why It Matters

Nicknamed "Jodi," the skeleton sold at Sotheby's in New York on October 27, 2026, for $47.3 million, making it the second most valuable dinosaur fossil ever auctioned. Because the specimen was recovered from private land in Montana, local laws granted ownership to the landowner, and federal preservation acts did not apply.

The private sale highlighted growing concerns within the scientific community. Representatives from the Society of Vertebrate Paleontology, including Kristina Curry Rogers, stressed that permanent placement in public institutions is critical for future research, as emerging technologies frequently allow scientists to gain fresh insights from specimens decades after their initial unearthing.
