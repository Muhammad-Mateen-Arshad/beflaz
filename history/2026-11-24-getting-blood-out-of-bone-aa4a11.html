Getting blood out of bone

Published on November 24, 2026

The Discovery

In a groundbreaking scientific achievement, researchers have identified blood-related molecules inside an ancient dinosaur fossil for the first time. The discovery stems from the remains of Aratasaurus museunacionali, a two-legged theropod belonging to the same family as tyrannosaurs, velociraptors, and modern birds. This creature lived roughly 115 million years ago during the Early Cretaceous period in present-day Brazil.

The fossilized right hind leg of the creature endured a remarkably lucky chain of events. It initially survived multiple explosive blasts from active operations inside a gypsum mine. It later survived the devastating 2018 National Museum of Brazil fire, which destroyed nearly 20 million items, because the leg was housed in a separate annex building untouched by the blaze. The dinosaur's scientific name reflects this survival story, translating to the "dinosaur born from the national museum fire."

What Scientists Found

Using electron microscopy, scientists examined the fossilized leg and discovered tiny oval-shaped structures in the vascular canals where blood vessels once ran. Subsequent chemical analysis confirmed the presence of heme inside these structures. Heme is an iron-containing ring molecule found within hemoglobin, the protein in red blood cells that carries oxygen throughout a living body.

While the team did not recover intact, living blood cells, they interpreted these mineralized ovals as preserved ancient red blood cells. Researchers hypothesize that the dinosaur was rapidly buried in an environment low in oxygen but rich in microbes. According to Professor Kliti Grice, microbial communities constructed a protective biofilm around the bone, which prevented it from fully decomposing.

Why It Matters

Finding intact heme-derived molecules inside a fossil that is 115 million years old offers extraordinary evidence of how organic compounds can persist over massive geological timeframes. Professor Grice described the detection of these preserved molecules in ancient red blood cells as an astonishing landmark discovery, offering fresh insight into the molecular preservation of prehistoric life.
