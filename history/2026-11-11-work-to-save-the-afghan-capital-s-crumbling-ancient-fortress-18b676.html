Work to save the Afghan capital's crumbling ancient fortress uncovers archaeological treasures

Published on November 11, 2026

The Discovery

High above the old city of Kabul, Afghanistan, efforts to restore the historic fortress of Bala Hissar have uncovered a rich tapestry of ancient history. Situated on the slopes of the Kuh-e-Sher Darwaza mountain ridge, the site—whose name means "High Fort" in Dari—has endured centuries of conflict, neglect, and political shifts. The Aga Khan Trust for Culture began planning a major conservation project in 2017, initiating physical work the following year to stabilize the crumbling fortifications and turn the location into a public archaeological park.

What Scientists Found

Excavations across five distinct trenches have revealed remarkable evidence spanning more than two millennia of human activity. Archaeologists working at the site, which includes a trench reaching 11 meters deep, have uncovered a wide range of artifacts and architectural remnants, including:

  • Ancient coins, pottery fragments, glazed Islamic ceramics, and oil lamps.
  • Fragments of Buddhist statues alongside the remains of a Buddhist monastery.
  • The ruins of a Mughal palace featuring wall murals, a throne-like structure, luxurious baths, and a star-shaped pool carved directly into the hilltop rock.
  • Traces of ancient human habitation, residential dwellings, and military structures.

Why It Matters

Bala Hissar occupies a critical location along a historic trade route connecting the Indian subcontinent to Central Asia. Positioned strategically to guard access routes to Kabul, control of the fortress historically meant control over the surrounding region. It has served various roles over time, acting as a royal residence, religious settlement, military stronghold, prison, administrative center, and arsenal from the first century B.C. through modern conflicts.

Despite heavy damage over the centuries—including destruction by British forces in 1879 and site leveling by U.S. troops in 2008—the ongoing project aims to preserve this multi-layered heritage. Beyond rebuilding defensive walls using local stone and metal supports, the long-term initiative provides training for Afghan archaeology students while preparing the site to host landscaped gardens, restaurants, and a museum.
