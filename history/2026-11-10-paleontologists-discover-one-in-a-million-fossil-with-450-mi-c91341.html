Paleontologists discover “one-in-a-million” fossil with 450-million-year-old soft tissue

Published on November 10, 2026

The Discovery

Paleontologists from the University of Oklahoma have identified an extraordinarily rare fossil containing soft tissue that dates back more than 450 million years. The specimen belongs to Dendrocrinus simcoensis, an extinct species of marine animal known as a crinoid. These delicate remains are more than 200 million years older than the earliest dinosaurs. The exact location where this fossil was originally excavated is not specified in the source; please check the official notice.

What Scientists Found

Crinoids are ocean animals related to modern starfish and sea urchins. Resembling underwater flowers with branching arms, they thrived in Earth's earliest coral reefs long before animals and plants became widespread on land. Although millions of crinoid fossils have been recovered over time, nearly all of them preserve only hard skeletons because soft body parts decay quickly after death unless preserved in rare, vacuum-like conditions.

Researchers Dr. Lena Cole and Dr. David Wright, both assistant curators at the Sam Noble Oklahoma Museum of Natural History, examined the specimen and discovered preserved tube feet. This represents only the second known instance of fossilized soft tissue in crinoids and the oldest ever recorded. In echinoderms, tube feet are small, flexible organs used to interact with currents and collect food particles from seawater.

Why It Matters

Examining these preserved structures provides researchers with direct evidence of ancient anatomical features that usually disappear from the fossil record. Key takeaways from the discovery include:

  • Insight Into Evolution: Comparing the ancient fossil with living crinoids showed major anatomical differences, indicating that feeding structures underwent substantial evolutionary changes over hundreds of millions of years.
  • Reconstructing Past Lifestyles: The shape, size, and arrangement of tube feet help scientists determine how extinct marine species fed and adapted to their specific habitats.
  • Understanding Early Oceans: The discovery helps paleontologists reconstruct marine ecosystems from the early Paleozoic Era—a period that began around 541 million years ago—and trace how adaptive evolution shaped the modern biosphere.
