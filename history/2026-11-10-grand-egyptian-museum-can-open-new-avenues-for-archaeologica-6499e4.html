Grand Egyptian Museum can open new avenues for archaeological cooperation with Kuwait, experts say

Published on November 10, 2026

Ancient trade and cultural ties between Egypt and the Arabian Peninsula are coming to the forefront as researchers highlight new opportunities for regional archaeological collaboration. Discussions surrounding the Grand Egyptian Museum demonstrate how modern conservation and research can shed light on thousands of years of shared heritage.

The Discovery

Physical artifacts found across the region reveal longstanding historical connections. Key discoveries include five Egyptian scarab seals uncovered on Kuwait's Failaka Island. These small, beetle-shaped objects were traditionally used in ancient Egypt for sealing and held symbolic or religious significance. Further across the Arabian Peninsula in Tayma, Saudi Arabia, archaeologists also located an ancient cartouche—an oval frame used to enclose a ruler's name—bearing the inscription of Pharaoh Ramses II.

What Scientists Found

Research into the Failaka Island scarab seals, which date back to the second millennium BC (around 4,000 years ago), focuses on determining how the items arrived in the Gulf. Studies indicate they may have been transported directly from Egypt or moved through secondary trading networks in regions like Syria. Additional evidence highlights ancient commercial routes that carried incense and spices from Oman and southern Arabia through present-day Kuwait, eventually reaching Egyptian royal palaces and temples.

Why It Matters

These ancient exchanges show that civilizations such as Dilmun are part of a broader shared heritage that still influences modern customs, food, and traditions like the Egyptian spring festival Sham el-Nessim. Scholars emphasize that the Grand Egyptian Museum features specialized laboratories that can collaborate with institutions like Kuwait University to restore artifacts and advance regional research.

Situated near the Great Pyramid of King Khufu in Giza, the museum guides visitors chronologically from prehistoric eras through the Old Kingdom and Late Period. By blending modern technology with historical collections—such as jewelry, tools, statues, and artifacts belonging to Tutankhamun—the institution creates a new model for preserving cultural history for future generations.
