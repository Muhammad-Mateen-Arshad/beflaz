Museum Fossils Reveal Giant New Dinosaur Hidden for 25 Years

Published on November 09, 2026

For a quarter of a century, a significant dinosaur specimen remained hidden in plain sight inside the Natural History Museum. Originating from Surrey, England, the fossilized remains were long assumed to belong to a previously recognized dinosaur species. However, recent reanalysis of the bones revealed that they belong to an entirely new herbivorous species named Paludohadros proudlovii.

The Discovery

The specimen was excavated in 2001 at the Ewhurst brickworks in Surrey, located within the Weald Clay Formation. Its genus name stems from the Latin word for "swamp," denoting its wetland habitat, while its species name honors Will Proudlove, the former brickworks manager who died before the excavation took place. After being donated to the Natural History Museum, the bones were originally categorized as Iguanodon or Mantellisaurus. That changed after scientists published a comprehensive study on Mantellisaurus in 2023, uncovering key anatomical differences that led researchers to reevaluate stored fossils and recognize the new species this year.

What Scientists Found

Paludohadros proudlovii was a large iguanodontian dinosaur estimated to have lived roughly 125 million years ago. Key physical traits and ecological details include:

  • Distinctive thumb spikes designed to ward off predators or pull down vegetation and break tough seeds.
  • Strong hind limbs enabling movement on either two legs or four legs.
  • A juvenile size measuring about 16 feet long—comparable to a rhinoceros—with fully grown adults potentially reaching twice that length.

The fossil was unearthed just 300 feet from where the predatory theropod Baryonyx walkeri was discovered in 1983. Because two Baryonyx teeth were found alongside the specimen, scientists suspect Baryonyx may have hunted or scavenged young Paludohadros dinosaurs.

Why It Matters

This identification underscores how much remains to be learned from existing museum collections, with additional museum fossils now being reassessed as potential Paludohadros specimens. Joining other recently named iguanodontians like Brighstoneus and Istiorachis, the find shows that southern England was once a thriving, vegetation-rich environment capable of sustaining multiple large plant-eating species.
