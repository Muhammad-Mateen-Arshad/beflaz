BC museum’s dinosaur celebrating 80,000,038th birthday

Published on November 14, 2026

The Courtenay and District Museum and Palaeontology Centre hosts a unique tradition: a birthday celebration for an ancient marine reptile fossil known as the Puntledge River elasmosaur, scientifically named Traskasaura sandrae. The event marks 38 years since the discovery of the 80 million-year-old fossil on Vancouver Island.

The Discovery

In November 1988, Mike Trask and his daughter Heather uncovered the fossil, marking the first discovery of its kind located west of the Canadian Rocky Mountains. Due to the animal's unusually long neck, excavators originally mistook its head for its tail. The finding sparked widespread interest in local paleontology, inspiring Mike's brother, Pat Trask, to spend decades collecting fossils and educating the public. It also led to the establishment of the Vancouver Island Paleontological Society in 1992, the first organization of its type in the province.

What Scientists Found

Elasmosaurs were marine reptiles that swam in late Cretaceous oceans roughly 85 million years ago. They featured small heads, bulbous bodies, and necks as long as their main torsos, giving them a physical appearance similar to descriptions of the Loch Ness monster. After Pat Trask found a second specimen, a 2025 scientific paper officially designated the new genus and species as Traskasaura sandrae. The paper was led by F. Robin O’Keefe from Marshall University alongside co-author Pat Trask, with the scientific name honoring the Trask family members who helped uncover the specimens.

Why It Matters

The initial find acted as a catalyst for regional paleontology, prompting hundreds of subsequent discoveries of previously unknown prehistoric creatures. In October 2023, the elasmosaur was designated as British Columbia's official provincial fossil. The region's rich fossil record has since drawn international researchers, including scientists from the University of Tokyo studying cephalopods and visiting scholars from Poland. To honor the fossil's role in local science, the museum has hosted an annual public birthday party featuring cake, crafts, and party hats for the skeleton every year since 2008.
