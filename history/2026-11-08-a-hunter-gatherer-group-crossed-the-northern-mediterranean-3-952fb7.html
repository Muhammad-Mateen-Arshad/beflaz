A hunter-gatherer group crossed the northern Mediterranean 30,000 years ago, bringing a new type of stone tool from northern Spain to southern Italy

Published on November 08, 2026

The Discovery

Archaeologists excavating Riparo Mochi, a Palaeolithic rock shelter on the Italian Riviera near the French border, have resolved a long-standing mystery surrounding early human movement across Europe. Positioned in a vital natural passage between Provence and Italy, the site was crucial for tracking how prehistoric populations moved eastward. Previous research presented a chronological puzzle, as the Noaillian stone-tool tradition appeared to be thousands of years older in southern Italy than at this northern gateway.

Excavations led by Stefano Grimaldi in a newly opened section uncovered an ancient hearth surrounded by animal remains and thousands of worked flint pieces. After charcoal samples from the hearth were stolen before testing could take place, researchers from the University of Trento and the University of Pisa turned to an alternative dating method. They selected a tiny marine snail shell of the genus Bittium found nearby. Laboratory testing at the University of Vienna yielded a calibrated age between 29,900 and 30,800 years ago, establishing that toolmakers arrived at the site about 30,000 years ago.

What Scientists Found

Analysis of the artifacts surrounding the hearth revealed detailed insights into how these Ice Age hunter-gatherers prepared equipment and processed resources:

  • Stone Implements: The hearth area contained 73 finished tools made from local flint, including weapon tips called microgravettes and small chisel-edged tools known as Noailles burins.
  • Microscopic Wear: Microscopic analysis showed that Noailles burins were used to cut meat and hide, scrapers shaped wooden shafts, and splintered stone pieces acted as wedges to split bone.
  • Long-Distance Travel: A portion of the flint originated from raw stone sources located more than 125 miles (200 km) away in both the Apennine mountains and Provence.

Why It Matters

The revised age fits smoothly into a statistical model analyzing 54 high-quality dates from across Western Europe. This chronological model demonstrates that the Noaillian tradition emerged in northern Spain around 34,000 years ago, moved across the Pyrenees and southern France, reached Riparo Mochi around 30,000 years ago, and continued into Tuscany by roughly 29,000 years ago. By fixing the local timeline, the findings clarify how hunter-gatherer cultures expanded along the northern Mediterranean coastline during the last Ice Age.
