A newly discovered South African dinosaur was a heavyweight even as a youngster

Published on November 14, 2026

The Discovery

Researchers have identified a newly discovered dinosaur species named Nyana rooiwalensis, which lived in present-day South Africa more than 200 million years ago during the Late Triassic period. The ancient animal belonged to the sauropodomorph group, the evolutionary branch that eventually gave rise to colossal, long-necked creatures such as Brachiosaurus and Diplodocus. The identification was made using a nearly complete skull alongside several other fossilized bones, a significant find given that sauropodomorph skulls from this era are rarely recovered in southern Africa.

What Scientists Found

A research team led by Atashni Moopen from South Africa's University of the Witwatersrand used powerful X-rays to construct a detailed 3D digital model of the skull. By comparing its structural traits with related sauropodomorphs, the scientists successfully determined where the species fits on the dinosaur family tree.

To investigate the individual's age and growth rate, the researchers examined thin slices of its limb bones under a microscope. Their key findings include:

  • The creature likely walked on two legs and weighed around 400 kilograms (880 pounds).
  • Despite its substantial weight, microscopic bone analysis showed it was still a youngster in at least its third year of life, growing rapidly at the time of its death.
  • By measuring the circumference of the thigh bone and comparing it to growth curves of related dinosaurs, scientists concluded that fully grown adults of this species likely reached multi-tonne body masses.

Why It Matters

The findings, detailed in the journal Royal Society Open Science, offer broad insights into prehistoric evolution. Beyond introducing a new species, the discovery provides essential data on early massopodan skull anatomy and demonstrates that rapid growth patterns developed early in sauropodomorph history. Furthermore, the fossil evidence points to a previously unknown biogeographical link between southern Africa and North America during the Late Triassic.
