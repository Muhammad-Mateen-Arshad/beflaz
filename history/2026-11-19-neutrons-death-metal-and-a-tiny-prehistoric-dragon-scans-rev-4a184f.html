Neutrons, death metal and a tiny prehistoric dragon: Scans reveal new horned reptile species from 156 million years ago

Published on November 19, 2026

The Discovery

In August 2022, an excavation team from the Aathal Dinosaur Museum uncovered a tiny fossil inside sandstone at Meilyn Quarry in Wyoming. The remains belonged to a creature that lived 156 million years ago during the Jurassic period. To reveal the structure hidden inside the rock, researchers used advanced scanning techniques. The investigation began after Pavel Trtik, a materials scientist at the Paul Scherrer Institute (PSI), left his business card during a family museum visit. Shortly after, the museum asked for his assistance in analyzing the mysterious American fossil.

What Scientists Found

Jointly led by Trtik and paleontologist Torsten Scheyer of the University of Zurich, an international team studied the specimen using both neutron and X-ray tomography. Neutrons reacted with hydrogen in the bone mineral hydroxyapatite, enabling researchers to distinguish the skeleton from the surrounding sandstone. After analyzing tens of thousands of tomographic images, the team identified:

  • Pelvic bones and portions of a hind leg
  • A body structure roughly 45 centimeters long, with the skull separated from the torso
  • A tiny skull measuring less than two centimeters across, complete with two dragon-like horns on the back

Published in Royal Society Open Science, the study suggests the creature died in a river, possibly following an attack by a larger predator. The exact species name is Not specified in the source; please check the official notice.

Why It Matters

The small reptile belongs to the choristoderes, a group of extinct reptiles whose evolutionary position has long remained uncertain. This fossil represents both the oldest known record of choristoderes in North America and the most complete early-branching specimen found to date. Significantly, tiny inner ear bones inside the preserved braincase show a striking resemblance to those of modern living reptiles, hinting that choristoderes may be more closely related to living reptiles than previous fossil evidence indicated.
