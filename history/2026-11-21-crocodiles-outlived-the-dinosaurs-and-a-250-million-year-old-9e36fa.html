Crocodiles outlived the dinosaurs, and a 250-million-year-old habit may explain why

Published on November 21, 2026

The Discovery

When a massive asteroid struck Earth 66 million years ago, non-avian dinosaurs perished, but ancient relatives of crocodiles managed to survive. A study led by Alfio Alessandro Chiarenza and Philip Mannion of UCL Earth Sciences reveals that their long-standing preference for warm climates helped them endure the catastrophe. By matching over 7,000 fossil records and roughly 130,000 modern species occurrences against 52 climate reconstructions spanning back 245 million years, researchers mapped where suitable weather existed throughout Earth's history.

What Scientists Found

Computer simulations recreating the decades and centuries following the asteroid impact showed that while suitable climates for non-avian dinosaurs dropped sharply, warm regions around the equator and subtropics remained livable for crocodile relatives. These areas stayed suitable across nearly all tested scenarios, including those with atmospheric ash, reduced sunlight, and increased carbon dioxide from volcanic eruptions in India.

The research highlighted several key findings about these animals and their history:

  • Rainfall was an even stricter requirement than temperature, with lineages returning to preferred moisture levels four times faster than to preferred warmth.
  • Survival was aided by non-climate factors, including smaller body sizes, cold-blooded metabolisms with low energy needs, flexible diets, and lives divided between land and water.
  • The ancient group was far more diverse than living species, featuring two-legged land predators, armored plant eaters, small pug-faced species, and giants over 2,200 pounds capable of eating a T. rex.
  • Despite this evolutionary diversity, their climate preference shifted only twice, such as when an extinct Australian lineage adapted to dry desert rainfall around 34 million years ago.

Why It Matters

The study shows that crocodiles historically survived massive environmental changes by relocating to favorable habitats. This finding has urgent implications today. As the planet warms again, living species need room to move, but human obstacles like cities, farms, and roads are making those migrations far more difficult.
