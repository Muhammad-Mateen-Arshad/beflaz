Lake Vostok: The ancient lake no one on Earth has ever seen

Published on October 11, 2026

The Discovery

Deep beneath two and a half miles of ice in East Antarctica lies Lake Vostok, an ancient environment that has been concealed for at least 15 million years. Although it is the 16th largest lake on Earth and the largest known subglacial lake in Antarctica, no human has ever seen it directly. The lake measures approximately 160 miles in length, 30 miles in width, and 3,000 feet in depth. Since its discovery, researchers have identified more than 350 smaller subglacial lakes throughout the Antarctic region.

Because of its extreme isolation, Lake Vostok remains completely untouched by human activity, plastics, or fossil fuels. Scientists mapped the lake and later utilized detailed bedrock data gathered in 2013, including information from IceBridge, to better understand how the Antarctic ice sheet flows and interacts with the rock beneath it.

What Scientists Found

Despite surface temperatures above the ice dropping to a freezing minus 76 degrees Fahrenheit, the water beneath remains liquid at around 26.6 degrees Fahrenheit. The massive layer of ice acts as an insulating blanket, trapping geothermal heat rising from within the Earth. Over thousands of years, meltwater from the overlying ice—which is roughly 700,000 years old—slowly replenishes the lake.

Analysis of frozen lake water revealed a complex microbial ecosystem. Researchers discovered two distinct types of organisms living in this environment:

  • Psychrophiles, which thrive in extreme cold.
  • Thermophiles, which require extreme heat to survive.

The presence of heat-loving thermophiles suggests that hydrothermal vents, akin to those found in hot springs, likely exist at the bottom of the lake.

Why It Matters

The unique aquatic environment of Lake Vostok serves as a terrestrial model for unexplored worlds in space. Moons such as Jupiter's Europa and Saturn's Enceladus feature subsurface oceans of liquid water under thick, icy shells. Studying Antarctica's subglacial lakes provides insight into what organisms might exist beneath the ice of outer space. Consequently, NASA views these subglacial lakes as a vital research focus for astrobiology and one of the final frontiers on Earth.
