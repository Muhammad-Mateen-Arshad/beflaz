Tagore has a dinosaur named after him. So does the man who dug it up

Published on November 07, 2026

The history of Indian palaeontology features a unique connection between literature and geology, sparked by a major fossil deposit uncovered in central India.

The Discovery

In 1958, geologists found ancient dinosaur bones near Pochampalli in present-day Maharashtra. This led to systematic excavations between 1960 and 1961 in the Pranhita-Godavari Valley. Conducted jointly by the Geological Studies Unit of the Indian Statistical Institute in Calcutta and University College London, the expedition was led by Pamela Lamplugh Robinson and funded by the Royal Society of London.

The research group, which included Indian palaeontologist Sohan Lal Jain, recovered around 300 bones from a single fossil deposit in the Lower Jurassic Kota Formation. The remains belonged to at least six individual plant-eating sauropods.

What Scientists Found

Because 1961 marked the birth centenary of poet Rabindranath Tagore, the Indian Statistical Institute's Scientific Council approved the name Barapasaurus tagorei in his honour. A formal description of the dinosaur was later published in 1975 by Sohan Lal Jain, T.S. Kutty, T.K. Roy Chowdhury, and Sankar Chatterjee.

  • Physical dimensions: The dinosaur was estimated to be 12 to 14 metres long and lived approximately 190 million years ago during the Early Jurassic period.
  • Name origin: The generic name means "big-legged lizard," inspired by a remarkably large thigh bone measuring roughly 1.7 metres.
  • Current location: The mounted skeleton is displayed at the Geological Museum of the Indian Statistical Institute in Kolkata.

Why It Matters

The 1961 expedition launched a 33-year career for Sohan Lal Jain at the Indian Statistical Institute. His extensive research on central Indian sauropods included analyzing a sauropod braincase from the Lameta Formation near Jabalpur to help classify prehistoric vertebrates. In 1995, palaeontologists Adrian Hunt, Martin Lockley, Spencer Lucas, and Christian Meyer named the genus Jainosaurus to recognize Jain's major contributions to palaeontology.

In 1997, Jain and Saswati Bandyopadhyay described another sauropod, originally named Titanosaurus colberti after American palaeontologist Edwin H. Colbert. In 2003, Jeffrey Wilson and Paul Upchurch reclassified it under the new genus Isisaurus to honour the Indian Statistical Institute. These linked discoveries demonstrate how scientific understanding evolves through systematic excavation, description, and ongoing recheck of historical finds.
