This 345-million-year-old fossil had scientists fooled for decades

Published on November 26, 2026

The Discovery

For nearly three decades, a 345-million-year-old fossil uncovered in Scotland in 1984 was viewed as one of the oldest known amniotes—the group of backboned animals that includes modern reptiles, birds, and mammals. Known by the scientific name Westlothiana lizziae and affectionately nicknamed "Lizzie," the specimen consists of an almost full skeleton encased within two crushed rock slabs. Following its formal scientific description in 1990, researchers considered it a key point of reference for understanding early land-dwelling life, primarily because its surface anatomy displayed distinct limbs with claw-tipped fingers and toes rather than fins.

What Scientists Found

A study published in Nature has upended decades of consensus. A team led by researchers from the American Museum of Natural History and the University of Oxford, alongside experts at the European Synchrotron Radiation Facility in Grenoble, France, utilized high-powered X-ray imaging to look inside the rock without damaging the fossil.

The interior scans unveiled unexpected features that overturned previous assumptions about the creature:

  • A primitive skull structure fundamentally different from early reptiles
  • Internal gills containing bone
  • A fish-like mouth lined with thousands of tiny teeth

These concealed traits demonstrate that Westlothiana lizziae was not an early land reptile, but rather an amphibious or aquatic creature belonging to a far more ancient evolutionary line.

Why It Matters

The reassessment changes how paleontologists trace the origin of land-based physical traits. Features traditionally tied to land adaptation—such as clawed digits and five-fingered hands—appear to have developed earlier in water-dwelling ancestors within the tetrapod lineage before vertebrates fully transitioned onto land.

This insight also impacts the interpretation of ancient footprint tracks. Fossilized trackways from the Devonian Period, dated to more than 359 million years ago, were previously assumed to belong to early amniotes due to impressions of clawed toes. Because aquatic relatives of tetrapods also carried claws, footprint tracks alone are no longer definitive proof of early amniote activity.
