[Research is Interesting! #44] A footprint about 70cm long was found. How much can we tell from a footprint?

Published on November 22, 2026

When studying ancient life, researchers rely on more than just physical body fossils such as bones, teeth, or shells. Traces left behind by prehistoric creatures, including footprint fossils, offer direct evidence of where animals moved across ancient landscapes.

The Discovery

In October 2026, Minamisoma City in Fukushima Prefecture announced the discovery of fossilized dinosaur footprints. More than 10 footprints were identified in a geological layer dating back roughly 160 million years to the Late Jurassic period. The largest of these footprints measured about 70cm long.

What Scientists Found

Because these impressions are trace fossils rather than body fossils, they contain no bone structure. According to the announcement, the tracks were likely left by sauropods—large, herbivorous dinosaurs known for their long necks—or similar animals. However, researchers have not made a definitive identification, and detailed investigation remains necessary.

Several key details have not been determined from the footprints at this stage of research:

  • The exact species of sauropod (Not specified in the source; please check the official notice).
  • The full body size of the dinosaur (Not specified in the source; please check the official notice).
  • The walking or running gait of the animal.
  • The total number of individual dinosaurs that passed through the area.

Why It Matters

The 70cm footprint has been identified as the largest Jurassic dinosaur footprint fossil confirmed in Japan. For comparison, reports indicate that the previous record holder for a Jurassic footprint in the country was an impression measuring approximately 33cm, discovered in Shimonoseki City, Yamaguchi Prefecture.

The initial research was carried out by a team including experts from the Minamisoma City Museum, the Fukushima Museum, and additional researchers from inside and outside the city. The discovery was announced jointly by the city and the museum, prior to peer review or publication in a formal scientific journal. The research team plans to continue its work by converting the footprints into 3D data for detailed shape analysis and completing a full academic evaluation.
