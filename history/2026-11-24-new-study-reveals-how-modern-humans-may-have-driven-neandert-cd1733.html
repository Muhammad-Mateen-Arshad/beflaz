New study reveals how modern humans may have driven Neanderthals to extinction

Published on November 24, 2026

For years, researchers have debated whether the demise of Neanderthals across Europe was tied directly to the expansion of our own species, Homo sapiens. A study published in the journal Science Advances sheds light on this ancient mystery through complex digital modeling.

The Discovery

Led by Marco Vidal-Cordasco of the University of Cantabria in Spain, a research team built a virtual model of prehistoric Europe. They integrated historical climate records and animal population trends to calculate available food sources between 55,000 and 27,000 years ago. Specific fossil excavation sites were not specified in the source; please check the official notice.

By executing thousands of computer simulations, the scientists evaluated how climate shifts, population scales, and the migration of Homo sapiens impacted Neanderthal survival rates.

What Scientists Found

The results revealed that environmental changes were usually insufficient to drive Neanderthals to complete extinction on their own. Key insights from the simulations include:

  • In 98% of scenarios where modern human populations stayed small, Neanderthals managed to survive despite climate-driven food shortages.
  • When Homo sapiens grew in numbers and expanded across the continent, Neanderthal extinction risks rose dramatically.
  • Neanderthal populations that maintained mobility and kept in contact with neighboring groups survived far longer than isolated communities.
  • Homo sapiens likely created intense competition for vital resources and blocked Neanderthals from re-occupying former habitats.

Why It Matters

The study highlights that advanced technology or superior intellect were not strictly necessary for modern humans to displace Neanderthals. Instead, the two events represent interconnected demographic processes where population sizes and group connectivity played a decisive role.

Although these computer models provide virtual evidence rather than absolute physical proof, they offer a compelling explanation for how modern humans contributed to the fall of Neanderthals.
