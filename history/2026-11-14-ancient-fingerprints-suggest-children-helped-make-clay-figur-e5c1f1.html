Ancient fingerprints suggest children helped make clay figurines 15,000 years ago

Published on November 14, 2026

The Discovery

Long before the widespread rise of farming villages, Natufian hunter-gatherers in the Levant developed a regional tradition of shaping clay. In a study published in PLOS ONE, researcher Laurent Davin and colleagues from institutions including the Hebrew University of Jerusalem, the University of Haifa, and the Israel Antiquities Authority examined prehistoric clay artifacts recovered from three archaeological sites in present-day Israel: Eynan-Mallaha, Hayonim Terrace, and el-Wad Terrace.

What Scientists Found

The researchers examined 154 modeled clay items from Natufian layers, distinguishing human-made modifications from natural clay pieces through marks like grooves, pinches, and tool or finger impressions. Their key observations include:

  • Fifteen recognizable figurines representing human or animal forms, alongside 139 modeled cylindrical fragments.
  • Clear prints preserved on two figurines and five cylinders.
  • A small human figure from the prehistoric settlement of Eynan-Mallaha featuring overlapping finger marks left while the material was still soft.

By measuring the spacing of the print ridges, the team estimated the age groups of the individuals who handled the soft clay. Narrower ridge spacing indicated the presence of a child who had not yet reached puberty. On the human figurine, a child's mark overlapped a print left by an older individual, such as a young adult or an adult woman, showing that multiple people touched the object before it dried.

Why It Matters

Workable clay offered ancient creators options that stone and bone carving did not, allowing makers to repeatedly add, pinch, or smooth out material. The surviving impressions confirm that children took part in shaping and coloring clay objects around 15,000 years ago. These rare physical marks offer insight into how cultural knowledge and artistic techniques may have been passed down within early settled communities.
