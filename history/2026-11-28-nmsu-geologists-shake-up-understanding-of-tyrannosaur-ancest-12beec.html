NMSU geologists shake up understanding of tyrannosaur ancestry

Published on November 28, 2026

The Discovery

In 1983, boaters uncovered fossilized bones near the Elephant Butte Reservoir in southern New Mexico. Excavations retrieved a partial jawbone that paleontologists initially attributed to a Tyrannosaurus rex. Decades later, a study published in 2024 challenged that assumption. Researchers affiliated with the New Mexico Museum of Natural History and Science proposed that anatomical differences and rock dating pointed to a distinct species named Tyrannosaurus mcraeensis, which they claimed predated T. rex by approximately 6 million years.

What Scientists Found

Geologists from New Mexico State University set out to verify the fossil's true age. Professor Jeffrey Amato, faculty emeritus Tim Lawton, and graduate student Emma Schantz discovered that the rock sample used in the 2024 study came from a completely different stratigraphic section than the fossil site itself.

To establish an exact timeline, the team analyzed zircon crystals extracted from a volcanic ash layer located extremely close to the dinosaur fossil in the Hall Lake Formation. Using high-precision uranium-lead zircon geochronology, the geologists determined that the site dates back 69.4 million years—making it several million years younger than the previously claimed 74 million years.

Why It Matters

Because T. rex lived roughly between 68 million and 66 million years ago, the revised 69.4-million-year dating places the fossil much closer to the known T. rex timeline. While the geologists noted that dinosaur experts must reevaluate the fossil material itself, their findings provide essential clarity:

  • The volcanic ash analysis firmly dates the site to the mid-Maastrichtian age.
  • The fossil remains may belong to a T. rex rather than a separate species.
  • If it is a different species or a T. rex, it suggests the T. rex timeframe or lineage may require scientific adjustment.
