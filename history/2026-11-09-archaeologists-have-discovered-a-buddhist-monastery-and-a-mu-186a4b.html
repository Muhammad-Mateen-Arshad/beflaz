Archaeologists have discovered a Buddhist monastery and a Mughal palace in Afghanistan

Published on November 09, 2026

A major restoration project at the ancient Bala Hissar Fortress in Kabul, Afghanistan, is shedding light on more than two millennia of historical events. Situated on the slopes of the Kuh-e-Sher-Darvaza mountain range overlooking Kabul’s Old City, the site is undergoing a large-scale, multi-year recovery effort implemented by the Aga Khan Trust for Culture.

The Discovery

Archaeological research at the high-altitude fortification has revealed surprising structural features beneath centuries of accumulated debris. Archaeologists, including Babak Rafie-Alavi, examined soil across five separate excavated trenches, revealing that the strategic complex concealed layered evidence of ancient settlements, administrative seats, and religious centers.

What Scientists Found

Excavations uncovered the physical remains of a Buddhist monastery as well as a palace complex from the Great Mughal dynasty. At the Mughal palace location, researchers identified decorative fresco fragments, a structure resembling a throne, and a star-shaped pool carved straight into the hilltop rock. In addition to architectural foundations, the team recovered several distinct artifacts:

  • Ancient coins and broken fragments of Buddhist statues
  • Glazed Islamic ceramic pieces and oil lamps
  • Various ceramic objects and residential building ruins

Why It Matters

Historically, Bala Hissar functioned as a crucial defensive fortress, palace, garrison, administrative center, arsenal, and prison. Positioned along key trade routes linking the Indian subcontinent with Central Asia, it has witnessed conflicts ranging from ancient times—used as early as the 1st century B.C.E.—to modern military operations. The complex suffered heavy destruction over time, including devastation during British military campaigns, the Soviet invasion, civil war, and U.S. military activities in 2008 that destroyed historical archaeological layers.

The current restoration initiative, supported by project director Mustafa Nuri, aims to preserve these historical traces while offering practical training to local archaeology students. Plans for the site include transforming the area into an accessible archaeological park with landscaped gardens, restaurants, and a museum. Government representatives from the Ministry of Information and Culture, such as spokesperson Hubayb Gufran, have affirmed the importance of safeguarding these ancient remnants as valuable sources of historical knowledge.
