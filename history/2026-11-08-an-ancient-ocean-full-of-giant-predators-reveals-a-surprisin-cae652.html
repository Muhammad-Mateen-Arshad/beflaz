An ancient ocean full of giant predators reveals a surprising twist in evolution

Published on November 08, 2026

The Discovery

Paleontologists from McGill University and the Centro de Investigaciones Paleontológicas have examined an exceptional set of fossils from central Colombia. Located in the Early Cretaceous Paja Formation near Villa de Leyva, this ancient marine ecosystem offers a clear look at a critical turning point in Earth's evolutionary record. Most fossils gathered from the site are housed at the Centro de Investigaciones Paleontológicas. The exact numerical age of the site is not specified in the source; please check the official notice.

What Scientists Found

The fossilized community, referred to as the Paja Biota, reveals that surviving Jurassic groups lived alongside emerging Cretaceous lineages. Biology professor Hans Larsson and PhD graduate Dirley Cortés proposed the term "Biotic Inflection" to describe this period when declining groups coexisted with diversifying new species. This indicates that major evolutionary transitions can occur without the widespread destruction typical of mass extinctions.

Researchers credit a unique set of environmental factors for creating this high level of biodiversity:

  • Warm greenhouse temperatures
  • High sea levels
  • Coastal waters filled with nutrients
  • New marine pathways connecting the eastern Pacific and the early Atlantic ocean across modern-day Colombia

Previous studies examining feeding relationships within the Paja Biota uncovered a complex food web containing an unusually large number of apex predators. The preserved wildlife includes giant marine reptiles like long-necked plesiosaurs and relatives of marine crocodiles, alongside sharks, turtles, and crustaceans.

Why It Matters

This finding presents a new model for understanding how global biodiversity changes over time. Instead of relying on mass extinction events to clear ecological space, life may undergo major shifts during periods of high environmental complexity and coexistence. To determine how common Biotic Inflections were across geological history, scientists state that additional fossil finds, refined fossil age estimates, and global geological comparisons will be required.
