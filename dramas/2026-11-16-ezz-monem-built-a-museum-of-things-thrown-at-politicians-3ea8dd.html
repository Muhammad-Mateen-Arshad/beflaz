Ezz Monem Built a Museum of Things Thrown at Politicians

Published on November 16, 2026

What's new

Egyptian artist Ezz Monem has introduced an ongoing art project titled the Museum of Disruptions, which examines how everyday items transform into political gestures when thrown at public figures. Its initial phase, Archive of Thrown Objects, was displayed at Conners Conners in Melbourne from July 2 to August 1, 2026. The installation showcases 36 reconstructed projectiles spanning 132 years of events from 1892 to 2024, presented as archival pigment prints inside custom blackwood frames stretching five meters across the gallery wall.

About the project

Monem was originally inspired by video footage of Iraqi reporter Muntadhar al-Zaidi hurling his shoes at US President George W. Bush in December 2008. The artist researched documented incidents and recreated the objects to photograph them as preserved museum artifacts. The collection references actions aimed at various political leaders, including Winston Churchill, Richard Nixon, Silvio Berlusconi, Justin Trudeau, Hillary Clinton, Donald Trump, and Keir Starmer.

Reconstructed items featured in the exhibition include:

  • A gingerbread biscuit thrown at British Prime Minister William Ewart Gladstone in 1892.
  • Yoghurt connected to the Greek practice of yaourtoma, used against Greek justice minister Haris Kastanidis in 2011.
  • A miniature Milan Cathedral souvenir statuette used to strike Silvio Berlusconi in 2009.
  • A mango thrown at Venezuelan President Nicolás Maduro in 2015 containing a written request for housing assistance.
  • An IKEA Lufsig wolf toy, a pig's head, cabbage, eggs, adult toys, and a wooden block.

What to expect

Monem's photographs isolate each projectile without showing the surrounding crowds or target politicians, focusing entirely on the object's altered meaning. Details regarding streaming series adaptations or televised coverage are not specified in the source; please check the official notice.

The artist plans to launch a second chapter of the Museum of Disruptions in 2027, which will explore collective acts of disruption and how newspaper reports archive such events.
