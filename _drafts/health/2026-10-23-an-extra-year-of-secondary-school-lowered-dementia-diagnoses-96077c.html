A study supported by the National Institutes of Health (NIH) indicates that an additional year of required secondary schooling during youth may lower the risk of developing dementia later in life, particularly for individuals facing higher genetic risk.

Key Takeaways

  • Research suggests an extra year of compulsory education was linked to a lower incidence of dementia over a 50-year period.
  • Individuals with genetic factors associated with Alzheimer's disease and related dementias experienced the greatest benefit from the extra schooling.
  • The education policy change was also connected to improvements in general health and socioeconomic factors.

What the Source Says

Researchers at the University of Wisconsin–Madison and the University of Southern California analyzed medical records from more than 100,000 people in the UK Biobank database. They evaluated the impact of a 1972 law in England, Wales, and Scotland that raised the minimum school leaving age from 15 to 16 for students born after September 1, 1957. This law raised enrollment for 16-year-olds by 14 percent.

Focusing on diagnoses made before December 2022, when participants reached age 65, the researchers found that the extra year of school reduced Alzheimer's disease and related dementia incidence over the next 50 years by 0.88 percent. The National Institute on Aging highlighted that these results show how lifestyle and educational factors can offset inherited genetic vulnerabilities.

Practical Notes

The study authors emphasize cautious interpretation because participant data was evaluated up to age 65. As the population ages and dementia becomes more common, the long-term differences between groups could change.
