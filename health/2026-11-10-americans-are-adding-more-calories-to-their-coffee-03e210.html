Americans Are Adding More Calories to Their Coffee

Published on November 10, 2026

Key Takeaways

Research suggests that while overall coffee consumption among American adults remained steady over a 15-year period, the number of daily calories added to coffee rose significantly. According to a study examining national dietary data, non-dairy creamer grew in popularity and became the primary contributor of calories added to coffee, ahead of sugar and dairy milk.

What the Source Says

Researchers at the Gerald J. and Dorothy R. Friedman School of Nutrition Science and Policy at Tufts University published an analysis in the Journal of the Academy of Nutrition and Dietetics based on dietary recalls from 30,000 U.S. adults aged 20 and older. The data was gathered across eight cycles of the National Health and Nutrition Examination Survey from 2003 to 2018.

The study found the following details regarding adult coffee habits:

  • Overall coffee intake held stable at approximately 13 ounces per day.
  • Energy intake from coffee increased from an average of 43 calories per day in 2003 to 60 calories per day in 2018.
  • By 2018, non-dairy creamer provided the highest daily caloric contribution among additives (18.5 calories), followed by sugar or syrup (16 calories) and dairy milk (11 calories).
  • Total non-dairy additions expanded from 3.65 grams daily in 2003 to 10.5 grams daily in 2018, driven largely by non-dairy creamers and plant-based milk alternatives.
  • Adults who logged fewer hours of sleep or worked longer hours tended to consume higher amounts of coffee.

Practical Notes

The study authors pointed out that while coffee consumption is associated with several health benefits, extra ingredients like creamers and syrups can add extra saturated fat and added sugars to an individual's diet. Lead study author Yongyi Pan noted that non-dairy creamers can be high in saturated fat, making them calorie-dense. Senior author Fang Fang Zhang emphasized that understanding what people add to their beverages helps researchers better examine the relationship between daily nutrition and broader health outcomes.

The researchers acknowledged a limitation in their work, noting that self-reported dietary recalls can introduce measurement error due to day-to-day changes in what people eat and drink. Personal calorie targets or specific dietary guidelines were not specified in the source; please check the official notice. You should always speak with your doctor to discuss your individual nutrition needs and health goals.
