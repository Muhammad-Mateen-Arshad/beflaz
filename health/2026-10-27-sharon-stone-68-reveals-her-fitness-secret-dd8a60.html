Sharon Stone, 68, Reveals Her Fitness Secret

Published on October 27, 2026

Maintaining an active lifestyle as we age does not always require spending hours lifting heavy weights in a gym. Actress Sharon Stone, 68, demonstrates how integrating movement into daily routines can support overall physical and mental well-being.

Key Takeaways

  • Short periods of daily movement and light resistance exercises can help reduce sedentary time throughout the day.
  • Public health guidance emphasizes the value of regular physical activity, functional strength, and balance work for older adults.
  • Fitness routines are personal and should focus on health, mobility, and self-acceptance rather than societal pressures.

What the Source Says

Sharon Stone relies on a mix of regular daily movement, light resistance, and structured workouts. During work breaks, she performs bodyweight movements such as leg lifts, push-ups, sit-ups, and back kicks. She also wears small weighted bracelets while at her computer desk and performs rhythmic arm exercises with light weights, sometimes spending about 15 minutes on arm strengthening. Her broader schedule includes five pool sessions a week lasting an hour and a half each, where she uses 2.5-kilogram weights on her ankles and arms for aquagym exercises.

Stone’s health philosophy connects physical activity with emotional balance, combining movement with yoga, breathing techniques, music, and meditation. Having survived a stroke and life-threatening brain hemorrhage in 2001, she focuses on overall well-being over extreme thinness or unrealistic beauty standards. Her diet emphasizes simple foods, including fruit, vegetables, and milk.

Practical Notes

According to guidance from the World Health Organization, adults are advised to get at least 150 to 300 minutes of moderate-intensity aerobic physical activity per week. For older adults, adding activities that focus on balance and functional strength can be particularly beneficial according to their physical abilities. Small actions—such as taking the stairs, walking, or standing up regularly during desk work—can help reduce sitting time for those who find long gym workouts difficult to schedule. Strength training supports mobility and independence as people age, but activities should always be adapted to individual fitness levels.

Please talk to your doctor before starting or changing an exercise program.
