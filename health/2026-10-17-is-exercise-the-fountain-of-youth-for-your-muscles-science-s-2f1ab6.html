Is Exercise the Fountain of Youth for Your Muscles? Science Says Maybe

Published on October 17, 2026

Key Takeaways

  • Research suggests exercise may help protect aging muscles by influencing a biological switch controlled by a gene regulator named DEAF1.
  • Experiments on older mice revealed that physical exertion reduced DEAF1 levels and improved cellular cleanup.
  • The optimal workout intensity, duration, and frequency for humans are not specified in the source; please check the official notice.

What the Source Says

A study led by Duke-NUS Medical School and published in the Proceedings of the National Academy of Sciences examined how aging impacts muscle health. Healthy muscles continually balance building new proteins and clearing out damaged ones, a process regulated by a cellular pathway called mTORC1. As time passes, mTORC1 can become chronically overactive.

Researchers identified that elevated levels of DEAF1, a gene regulator, play a major role in this imbalance. High DEAF1 activity allows damaged proteins to build up by interfering with natural cellular cleanup. Study senior author Hong-Wen Tang, PhD, stated that identifying DEAF1 clarifies how age-related stress damages muscle cells, showing that age-related decline involves a loss of efficiency in tissue maintenance machinery.

To see how physical activity affects this process, researchers placed older mice on treadmills and resistance wheels. The exercise activated FOXO proteins, which suppressed DEAF1 and restored balance to the mTORC1 pathway, enabling better removal of damaged cellular waste. While these findings offer a biological explanation for physical activity's protective effects, scientists emphasize that targeting DEAF1 has not been proven to reverse muscle aging in humans.

Practical Notes

The research does not provide specific instructions regarding an ideal exercise regimen for older adults. For general strength maintenance, the source notes that adults can combine regular resistance training with aerobic exercise and adequate rest. Always talk to your doctor before starting any new exercise program.
