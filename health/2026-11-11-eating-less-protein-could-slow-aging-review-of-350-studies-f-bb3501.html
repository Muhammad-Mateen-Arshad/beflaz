Eating less protein could slow aging, review of 350 studies finds

Published on November 11, 2026

Key Takeaways

  • A scientific review suggests that consuming less protein may support metabolic health and slow biological aging.
  • Researchers identified key biological processes, including hormone responses, that link protein intake to cellular health.
  • Higher protein intake remains important for maintaining muscle strength, mobility, and preventing falls in older adults.

What the Source Says

An evaluation of over 350 scientific papers published in the Cell Press journal Cell Press Blue examined how reducing protein intake affects health. The review's corresponding author, Dudley Lamming of the University of Wisconsin-Madison, alongside a colleague, noted that while active individuals benefit from protein for muscle growth, less active individuals may consume more than needed.

Research in organisms such as flies and rodents shows that limiting protein can extend lifespan without reducing overall calories. In human clinical trials, research suggests that reducing protein intake can lead to lower body fat, weight loss, and improved fasting blood sugar levels. While this does not prove protein restriction extends human lifespan, it indicates an impact on aging processes.

The review highlighted fibroblast growth factor 21 (FGF21), a hormone that increases when dietary protein is restricted. Research suggests FGF21 boosts energy expenditure, lowers inflammation, and aids blood sugar regulation. Studies in mice revealed longer lifespans with elevated FGF21, especially in males. While human protein restriction increases FGF21, longevity outcomes in humans are not established.

The review also discussed specific amino acids, including methionine, isoleucine, and valine, which act as signals for metabolism. The full impact of excessive intake of these amino acids was not specified in the source; please check the official notice.

Practical Notes

Determining protein needs involves balancing metabolic health with physical strength. Other studies demonstrate that higher protein intake helps older adults protect muscle mass and reduce mobility loss or fall risks, particularly when paired with exercise. Recent US dietary guidance encourages 1.2 to 1.6 grams of protein per kilogram of body weight daily (0.5 to 0.7 grams per pound).

Always talk to your doctor before making changes to your dietary protein intake or meal plan.
