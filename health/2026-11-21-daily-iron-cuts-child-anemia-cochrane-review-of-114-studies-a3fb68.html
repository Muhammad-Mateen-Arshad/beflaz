Daily Iron Cuts Child Anemia, Cochrane Review of 114 Studies Finds

Published on November 21, 2026

Key Takeaways

  • Research suggests daily iron intake probably lowers the likelihood of iron deficiency anemia in children and young people up to 19 years old.
  • The analysis indicates that iron supplements made no difference to growth measures like height and weight, and had no effect on cough, fever, or respiratory illness.
  • Investigators highlight that the quality of evidence is low to very low, so the findings should not be interpreted as a recommendation for routine iron supplements for all youths.

What the Source Says

An updated systematic review published in the Cochrane Database of Systematic Reviews and led by researchers at Cornell University gathered data from 114 studies including 50,325 children and adolescents. The review evaluated daily oral iron delivered as capsules, tablets, or syrup. The authors concluded that iron given alone or alongside folic acid probably reduces the risk of iron deficiency anemia, while iron combined with other minerals and vitamins may also lower the risk.

Iron deficiency anemia happens when the body does not have enough iron to produce hemoglobin, the red blood cell protein responsible for carrying oxygen. This condition can lead to fatigue, weakness, and potential developmental effects in early childhood. Side effects reported in the studies were mostly mild, with darker stools occurring frequently. While iron combined with other nutrients probably raised the occurrence of vomiting, the effect of iron alone on constipation was judged to be very uncertain.

Practical Notes

Study authors Saurabh Mehta and Kripa Rajagopalan emphasized that while supplements perform as intended, the overall certainty of evidence remains low or very low, meaning further studies could change these conclusions. Because of this, the authors state that the evidence does not support universal iron supplementation. Public health strategies often combine targeted supplementation with broad food fortification—such as adding iron to flour or rice—and biofortified crops like high-iron beans to help improve dietary iron intake in communities.

If you have questions about nutrition, nutrient deficiencies, or supplementation for yourself or family members, please talk to your doctor.
