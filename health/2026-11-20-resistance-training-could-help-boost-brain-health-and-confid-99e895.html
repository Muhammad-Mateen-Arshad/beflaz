Resistance training could help boost brain health and confidence in older adults

Published on November 20, 2026

Staying active is vital as we age, helping to maintain physical independence and daily function. Research reviewed by Science X suggests that engaging in strength training may support both physical movement and certain thinking skills in older adults.

Key Takeaways

  • Research suggests a 24-week resistance training program can subtly improve performance on specific mental tasks among older adults with mild cognitive impairment.
  • Strength exercises help train the brain and nervous system to coordinate muscle movements more effectively.
  • Regular training may support brain health indirectly by aiding blood pressure control, insulin response, and inflammation reduction.
  • Improved physical strength can make routine tasks easier, encouraging older adults to stay active and socially connected.

What the Source Says

A study by Dayane Tays da Silva et al., published in The Journal of nutrition, health and aging, examined 162 adults aged 65 and older who had signs of mild cognitive impairment. Researchers compared gentle activity with a 24-week resistance training program. Exercises gradually increased in difficulty, eventually incorporating balance challenges on unstable surfaces like foam pads.

By the study's end, the strength-training group achieved slightly higher scores than the gentle-exercise group on two mental tasks: immediately repeating a series of numbers and listing words starting with the letter A in one minute. Scores were similar between groups when remembering details from a story. Research suggests it remains uncertain whether these small test differences influence daily living or affect the risk of developing dementia.

When starting strength training, nervous system adaptations help create a "muscle map," making it easier to gauge effort and positioning. Furthermore, muscle contractions release myokines, which send signals throughout the body. Strength training can also lower blood pressure and improve insulin sensitivity, addressing factors linked to dementia risk.

Practical Notes

Functional movements, such as standing up from a chair or carrying groceries, draw directly on the strength built during resistance exercise. Feeling physically capable can make it easier to go out, shop, or join social activities. The source highlights that while more women live with dementia globally, fewer than one in four adults engage in muscle-strengthening exercises at least twice a week.

Specific customized exercise routines are not specified in the source; please check the official notice.

Always talk to your doctor before starting any new exercise program.
